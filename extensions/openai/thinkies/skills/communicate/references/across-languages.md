# Across Languages

Fits when: the piece crosses rhetorical traditions, or a first language other than the piece's own shapes its prose

Self-interrogation reference. Read before adapting prose across rhetorical traditions, before bridging a thought that resists expression in the target language, and before "correcting" grammar from the writer's first language (L1) as it carries into a second (L2). Questions replace a fixed taxonomy.

## How to hold these patterns

Rhetorical traditions are documented tendencies, not rules any writer must follow. Hold the patterns below as possibilities to ask about, never as labels to assign. A writer is a person, not a statistic: ask which tradition the piece works in, and never infer it from the language the writer or the audience speaks.

## Self-interrogation questions

### Which organizational logic does this prose use?

Linear-deductive order, thesis first, evidence below, conclusion last, is one tradition among several, and the one many drafts drift toward unasked. Which logic is this piece built on, and would a change of order move it into another without the user seeing that change?

**Parallel elaboration.** The argument advances through coordinated restatement, and the thesis emerges from the accumulated pattern. Repetition carries persuasive force rather than redundancy: a paragraph saying X, then X with variation, then X in a parallel construction is doing the argument. Arabic persuasive prose is one tradition that builds this way.

**Dialectic.** Thesis, antithesis, synthesis: the writer earns a position by inhabiting its opposite. A passage arguing against itself before resolving is the argument. The French academic *dissertation* is one tradition that builds this way.

**Indirect implication.** Introduce, develop, turn, bring together. The conclusion is often implied: an essay that never states its thesis but arranges evidence so the thesis is unmistakable makes its strongest claim by leaving it unstated. Japanese *ki-shō-ten-ketsu* is one tradition that builds this way.

**Scaffolded accumulation.** Define everything, qualify exhaustively, then commit. The scaffolding is the rigor, and compressing it destroys what was being demonstrated. German academic prose is one tradition that builds this way.

**Spiral recursion.** Themes return at different angles, and narrative carries argumentative weight. An argument that returns to its central image three times, each turn deepening, is working by method rather than repeating itself. Written prose that draws on oral storytelling traditions often builds this way.

When prose feels "foreign," which tradition is it working in? Name it before adjusting anything.

### What does each hedge calibrate?

Which hedges change what the writer commits to, and what does each of the others carry?

- "It's worth noting," "it's important to note," "in essence": what does the phrase carry, such as a stance, a rhythm, or a courtesy the form needs, if anything?
- "May," "might," "perhaps" attached to a claim whose warrant is uncertain: calibration.
- Japanese sentence-final particles (*ne*, *yo*, *kana*): calibration English cannot replicate grammatically.
- French or German subordinate-clause accumulation bounding a claim: calibration, which one clause cannot hold.

### Is L1 grammar carrying through L2 prose?

When it does, ask what the L1 pattern encodes that L2 cannot.

**Spanish ser vs estar.** Two verbs for "to be." *Es triste* = sadness as identity. *Está triste* = sadness as state. English collapses both to "is sad." If the writer means the temporal distinction, English needs the extra word: "feels sad right now," "is a sad person."

**Spanish tener + emotion.** *Tengo miedo* = "I have fear." Emotion is carried, not inhabited. "I am afraid" merges self and feeling. What, if anything, changes when *tengo miedo* is "corrected", and has the user seen that change?

**Japanese sentence-final particles.** One syllable encodes epistemic and relational stance. *Ne* proposes shared knowledge. *Yo* asserts. *Kana* mitigates. English needs a separate clause or tag question; the particle is structurally lost.

**Japanese implied subject.** The audience infers who acts from context. Adding "I" or "they" makes action explicit but removes the high-context quality. Where does the audience need the actor named, and where does the implication do the work?

**Arabic agentless construction.** A verb without an explicit agent may encode natural or systemic causation. English passive "it was done" implies a hidden human; the Arabic does not.

**French imparfait.** *Je mangeais* = experienced from within duration. *J'ai mangé* = viewed from outside as completed. "Was eating" / "ate" approximates but loses precision.

**German modal particles.** *Doch*, *ja*, *eben*, *halt*, *mal* carry contextual and relational weight. *Komm doch mit* = "come along [you know you want to]." English cannot say it in one word.

On seeing one of these patterns, ask: what does the writer mean? If they mean what the L1 encodes, the L2 needs annotation, not correction.

### Does this concept have a clean target-language equivalent?

Some do not.

- *Saudade* (Portuguese): a present-tense relationship with absence. Not nostalgia. Not grief. Longing that knows return may be impossible.
- *Hiraeth* (Welsh): longing for a place, sometimes one never visited or no longer existing. The place is the focus, not the relationship.
- *Wabi-sabi* (Japanese): beauty located in impermanence and incompleteness. Not "rustic charm."
- *Dor* (Romanian): longing tinged with grief. Heavier than *saudade*.
- *Jugaad* (Hindi): improvised, resource-constrained workaround that gets the job done. Contested in usage.

Where no clean mapping exists, would a gloss, the source word kept, or a phrase serve the audience better than the nearest English word?

### What does target-language rendering destroy? Is the loss worth the gain?

Every cross-tradition adaptation costs something. What does this one cost?

- Parallel restatement → hierarchical evidence. Lost: the felt weight of repeated pattern; the audience's experience of extracting the thesis.
- Dialectic → thesis-first. Lost: the demonstration of inhabiting the counter-position.
- Implied → explicit. Lost: the audience's participation; the dignity of implication.
- Scaffolded → compressed. Lost: the intellectual landscape visible before commitment.
- Spiral → linear. Lost: recursive depth; ecosystem framing.

Where the cost matters, offer a third option beside adapting and keeping: the source structure with a framing note for the target audience.

### Is the gap I'm about to fill doing rhetorical work?

Some silences are structural.

- Intentional omission sharpens focus.
- Strategic silence lets the audience complete the thought. The unsaid can land harder than the said.
- High-context traditions distribute meaning between text and shared knowledge, and explaining what the audience already shares can talk down to them.

Before adding content, ask: what does this gap do for the audience?

## Questions before any change

- Which choices in this piece are tradition-specific, and does the draft treat any one tradition's norms as the unmarked baseline?
- Where L1 grammar carries into L2, what does the pattern encode, and has that been diagnosed before anything is treated as a problem?
- For each hedge, what does it calibrate or carry in this instance?
- For each concept without a clean mapping, how does the draft carry it: by the nearest target-language word, a gloss, or the source word kept?
- Which statistical tendency, if any, is the draft treating as if it decided this writer's choices?

Every structural change to the user's prose falls under the authorship line in [baseline](./baseline.md): the user sees it.
