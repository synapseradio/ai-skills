# Align

Read this every run. Alignment is a conversation with the user, kept in a state file, that works through a wheel of eight rings. It moves inward from what brought the piece on to the point, then outward again as the piece is composed. Every later step reads its answers: Diagnose names risks on the rings, the rubric takes its questions from them, and Score sets each answer about the draft beside them.

## The wheel

The first four rings lead inward to the center, the point. Rings five to eight compose outward from it. Each ring below gives the question in plain words, what in a request usually answers it, and moves that help a user who cannot answer yet.

### 1. Incitement

- ask: "What brought this on? What happened, or is coming, that makes this piece needed now, and what rides on it?"
- infer: an event the request names, such as a death, a deadline, a dispute, a birthday, or a change in the code.
- lead: ask what happens if it is never written; ask what set it off.

### 2. Setting

- ask: "Who is this between, and where will it be met? Your relation to whoever meets it (an official, a friend, a stranger, a public figure, an animal), the place (an office, a street wall, a party, a courtroom, a feed, a log), and whether it speaks to one person, to a group as one body, to many one at a time, is overheard, or is kept for no one."
- infer: a named recipient or venue.
- lead: offer two settings and what each changes. A letter to a council office and one to a neighbour say the same thing in different words.

### 3. Audience and purpose

- ask: "Who meets it, and how: read, heard, glanced at, filed? What do they already bring, and what do they come to it for? If it is kept for no one, what do you come to it for?"
- infer: a form with one conventional audience and purpose. A cook at the stove wants the dish; a maintainer in the log wants why the code changed.
- lead: offer two audiences, or two purposes one audience might bring, and what each changes.

### 4. Point

The point is where the audience's purpose is met: what they reach, and hold, can do, feel, decide, or are bound to once there. It is a destination. The piece may take them there directly or let them arrive. For a piece kept for no one, the purpose is the writer's own.

- ask: "Where should they end up? Once they reach the end, what do they hold, or what can they do, feel, decide, or are they bound to, that they could not before? Should the piece take them there directly, or let them arrive?"
- infer: attached text shows where it takes its audience; a form sometimes fixes it, as a contract binds and a recipe ends at the dish. New work rarely lets it be inferred.
- lead: ask what the audience should do or say right after; offer two destinations, each with a closing line that shows it; ask for one moment or detail the arrival cannot do without; offer an exploratory sketch, kept in the state files and out of the artifact.

### 5. Material

- ask: "What does it rest on (names, events, details, terms), and what may I invent?"
- infer: attached text; an explicit permission to invent.
- lead: ask for everything that comes to mind, unsorted; sort it back as given, to check, and missing.

### 6. Form

- ask: "What kind of piece is it, whose conventions does it keep or break on purpose (a genre, a tradition, a period, a house style), and what does it promise its audience: an account, a story, a performance, an instrument, a game?"
- infer: a form the user names or the text plainly shows. Never infer a tradition from the language anyone speaks.
- lead: ask for an example they admire and name back its conventions; offer two shapes and what each costs.

Whether the piece is true, a story, satire, or a lie told on purpose is set here, in what the form promises. Record it as the answer to this ring, and let every later step take it as given.

### 7. Voice

- ask: "Who speaks, from where, and how should it sound?"
- infer: attached text carries its voice; some forms fix it.
- lead: ask for a sample; show one line in two registers.

### 8. Extent

- ask: "How much ground does it cover, what is in and out, and in what parts and order?"
- infer: the text in hand for an edit; an occasion's limit; an outline.
- lead: ask about time or place limits; offer a short and a long shape and what each leaves out; for long work, propose the skeleton as a list to cut, move, or add to.

## When alignment runs

A ring is open when nothing answers it, or when the two-sketch test splits on it. A ring whose sketches differ only in wording is settled by inference and stated back.

Run the conversation when any of rings 1 to 4 is open, when any choice the user has not made would steer the draft, or when the work runs past one sitting.

A quick change to text in hand runs no conversation. Infer the rings from the text, and state every inferred ring back in the closing line of the reply, one short clause each, so the user can correct any of them. Where the two-sketch test splits on a quick change, ask about each split alongside the change, one question each, and leave each part a split touches as the user wrote it.

## The two-sketch test

For a ring in doubt, write a one-line sketch of the piece under each of two plausible answers. Where the sketches differ in whom it is for, where it takes its audience, its form, or what it presents as the user's, the ring is open: ask. Where they differ only in wording, keep the inference and state it back.

**Contrast: a split sketch that goes to the user**

- antipattern: two sketches for a note about broken heating, "a formal notice that the heating has failed and repair is due within the period the tenancy sets" and "a friendly note asking when the engineer can come", then a draft of the friendly note.
- pattern: the same two sketches, then "Should this read as a formal notice you could point to later, or as a friendly note that keeps things easy with Sam?"
- observe: the sketches take the landlord to different places, bound in one and asked in the other; the antipattern picks one, and the pattern lets the user pick.
- shared: the same request and the same two sketches.
- contrast: a split settled in the draft against a split taken to the user.
- look for: two sketches that differ in audience, destination, form, or what they present as the user's, with no question after them.

## Pacing

Ask one question per message. Take it from the outermost open ring. Where no ring is open, take it from the open choice that would steer the next unit of the draft. Every user message updates every ring it touches, inner rings included, whichever ring the question was about.

A ring the user's words already answer is never asked. State it back in one line inside the next question.

**Contrast: a ring stated back, not asked**

- antipattern: after "Can you write the poster for our street party on Saturday? It goes on the lamp posts on Elm Road.", the reply "Who will see the poster, and where will it hang?"
- pattern: "Neighbours on Elm Road, catching it on a lamp post before Saturday. Once they've read it, what should they do: turn up, bring a dish, or move their cars off the road?"
- observe: the request already says where the poster hangs and who passes it; the pattern states that back and spends its one question on the point, the ring still open.
- shared: the same request, one question each.
- contrast: asking a ring the request answers against stating it back.
- look for: a question whose answer sits in the user's last message.

Where the user cannot answer, lead with the moves listed under the ring: offer contrasting readings, ask for an example, ask what should happen after. Never guess on the user's behalf.

**Contrast: leading when the user cannot answer**

- antipattern: the user writes "I don't know, just something nice for Dad's retirement", and the reply says "I'll end it on a toast to his next adventure."
- pattern: "Two ways to leave the room: 'Here's to thirty years of Monday mornings you'll never have to see again' leaves them laughing; 'Every one of us learned the job from you' leaves them grateful. Is either close, or is it somewhere else?"
- observe: the antipattern fills the point for the user; the pattern shows two destinations as closing lines and lets the user's answer set the point.
- shared: the same retirement speech, the same open point.
- contrast: a destination chosen for the user against two offered to them.
- look for: a ring the user said they could not answer, recorded as inferred.

## Direction

Before a choice the user has not made enters the draft, run the two-sketch test on it. Where it splits, ask. The draft does not carry the choice until the user answers; until then the choice waits in the open choices table of the alignment file, or in the ledger's open decisions for long work.

Where the user hands you the invention, "make up the names" or "you pick the ending", the handing over is their decision. Invent within it, and name each invented fact back to the user.

## State

Keep one alignment file per piece, `align.md`, built from [align-template](../assets/align-template.md). Each ring holds its answer, its source, and the date it last changed.

- `said`: the user's words give it, or give part of it; name the part still open in the answer.
- `shown`: the text in hand shows it.
- `inferred`: you drew it from the request or the form and stated it back; add `, confirmed` once the user accepts it.
- `open`: nothing answers it yet.

Update the file after every user message, and read it before every step of the loop. Long work also keeps a ledger, from [ledger-template](../assets/ledger-template.md), as [units-and-ledger](./units-and-ledger.md) describes.

## Storage

Keep the alignment file, the ledger, and the drafts of each piece in `${CLAUDE_PLUGIN_DATA}/communicate/<piece-slug>/`, where the slug is two to five lowercase words joined by hyphens that name the piece. Create the folder when it is missing. Where that path is not absolute, ask the user once where to keep them, and use that place for the rest of the work. The finished artifact goes where the user names.
