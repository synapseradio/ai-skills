# Arrange

Fits when: the piece has sections or parts whose order is still open to choice

Order sections, arguments, and paragraphs so the sequence serves the purpose.

## Diagnostic Question

Ask: Is the ordering of arguments, sections, or paragraphs purposeful? Could someone in the audience explain why this comes before that? Where, if anywhere, would rearranging change nothing?

## Instructions

1. **Distinguish arrangement from arc** -- Arc is about pull: what does the audience want to know, and when do they get it? Arrangement is about logical ordering: what must come first to make what follows possible? A piece can have strong arc (compelling pull) and weak arrangement (sections in arbitrary order), or strong arrangement (clear logical progression) and weak arc (nothing drawing the audience forward). Both matter; they do different work.

2. **Identify the ordering principle** -- Every arrangement implies a logic. Name it:
   - **Dependency** -- A requires understanding B, so B comes first
   - **Chronological** -- Events in the order they occurred
   - **Priority** -- Most important first (inverted pyramid)
   - **Accumulative** -- Each section builds on the previous, compounding understanding
   - **Comparative** -- Alternatives presented side by side for evaluation
   - **Problem-solution** -- Problem established, then solution proposed
   - **General-to-specific** or **specific-to-general** -- Zoom in or zoom out
   - **Dialectical** -- A position, its opposite, then what reconciles them
   - **Parallel restatement** -- The claim returns in varied form until the pattern carries it
   - **Delayed turn** -- Introduce, develop, turn to something apparently unrelated, then bring the parts together

   If no ordering principle is discernible, ask whether the arrangement is arbitrary.

3. **Test the ordering** -- For each major section or argument, ask: does this depend on something that comes after it? Could someone arriving at this point follow without backtracking? The prerequisite trace from [clarify](./clarify.md) applies at section level, not just sentence level.

4. **Check for buried leads** -- The most important point sometimes hides in the middle or end because the writer discovered it last. Writing order is not reading order. Where does the audience's purpose, rather than the order of discovery, place the point?

5. **Evaluate paragraph-level progression** -- Within sections, what work does each paragraph do that the previous one could not? Where two paragraphs could swap without consequence, what does each do for the section's purpose that the other does not?

6. **Ask which tradition the ordering follows** -- Ordering conventions vary across rhetorical traditions. Thesis-first deduction is one, common in Anglo-American academic and business writing. A dialectical movement, as in the French dissertation, is another. Parallel restatement, as in Arabic persuasive prose, is another. A delayed turn, as in ki-shō-ten-ketsu, is another. Which one does this piece follow, and did the user choose it? Read [across-languages](./across-languages.md) before changing the order of prose a tradition shapes.

7. **Hold the order across levels** -- When sections nest more than two levels deep, or the work runs past one sitting, keep the order in the skeleton of [units-and-ledger](./units-and-ledger.md), and record each change to it as a decision.

## Questions

- For each major section or argument, can I name the ordering principle this piece is using — dependency, chronology, priority, accumulation, comparison, problem-solution, general-to-specific, specific-to-general, dialectic, parallel restatement, delayed turn?
- For each section, does it depend on a concept introduced earlier in the piece, or does it reference something that arrives later? Could someone in the audience at this point follow without backtracking, and where they cannot, is the delay a choice the form makes, as a delayed turn or a mystery does?
- What is the strongest claim in the piece, and where is it placed? What placed it there: the audience's purpose, or the order in which the writer found it? Where the form leaves the claim implied, does the arrangement make it unmistakable?
- Within sections, does each paragraph earn its place by doing work the previous paragraph could not? If two paragraphs could swap without consequence, what is each one doing that the other is not?
- Which tradition's ordering does the piece follow, and would a change of order carry it into another tradition without the user seeing that change?

## Quality Criteria

When the arrangement is sound:

- [ ] The ordering principle for the piece is nameable.
- [ ] Each section depends only on what has come before it in the piece, or the forward dependency is a choice the form makes.
- [ ] The strongest claim sits where the audience can meet it without backtracking through the writer's discovery process.
- [ ] Each paragraph does work the previous paragraph could not, or repeats by a method the form uses, such as parallel restatement or a refrain.
- [ ] Where the ordering follows a tradition the audience may not expect, the user has decided whether to name it, keep it, or adapt it.
