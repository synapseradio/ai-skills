# Rhythm

Fits when: the piece will be heard or read aloud, its sound matters, or the user points out prose that reads as machine-made

Choose sentence length and structure so the rhythm does what the piece needs. A run of sentences at one length often marks a draft nobody listened to. A litany, a refrain, a list of steps, or a voice an earlier part already set may want exactly that run, so ask what the rhythm is for before changing it.

## Diagnostic Question

Where, if anywhere, does sentence length cluster? Where, if anywhere, does the rhythm flatten, and does the piece mean it to? Which long sentences, if any, carry a build, and which, if any, trail off under their own weight?

## Pacing questions

- **Length within a paragraph.** Where, if anywhere, do sentences sit at nearly one length, and does the form or the moment call for that evenness?
- **Three in a row.** Where, if anywhere, do three or more sentences in a row sit within two words of each other in length, and what would change if one of them grew or shrank?
- **Length across the piece.** Where, if anywhere, do three paragraphs in a row sit at nearly one length, and does the form set that length?
- **The end of a long sentence.** Which clause lands last in each long sentence, and is it the one the sentence builds toward, or a qualifier it trails into?
- **A short sentence after a long one.** What does it do: close, reverse, accent, or land a punchline? Where it does none of these, would it read better merged?
- **Fragments.** Does each fragment continue the sentence before it, set apart for accent, carry the form's own grammar, or stand as a slip?
- **Dashes and colons.** Does each dash interrupt or pivot, and each colon announce what follows? Where one does neither, would a comma serve?

## Periodic vs loose

A **periodic** sentence delays the main clause, so the meaning lands at the end. It builds suspense and emphasis.

**Contrast: main clause held to the end**

- antipattern: "Nobody moved, though the kettle had boiled twice and every coat hung ready by the door."
- pattern: "Though the kettle had boiled twice, and though every coat hung ready by the door, nobody moved."
- observe: the pattern holds the main clause back, so the stillness lands after the audience has felt how ready everything was.
- shared: the same words and the same facts.
- contrast: where the main clause sits.
- look for: a sentence that spends its turn in its first words when the piece wants its audience to wait for it.

A **loose** (cumulative) sentence puts the main clause first and adds modifiers. It reads as conversational and additive.

After moving a clause, reread what each remaining clause now modifies.

Loose is the unmarked English form. Use periodic when a sentence makes a single point you want to land hard. Use loose when accumulating texture.

## Parataxis vs hypotaxis

**Parataxis** places clauses side by side without ranking them. It reads as blunt and oral. **Hypotaxis** ranks clauses through subordination. It reads as analytical, layered, contemplative.

**Contrast: naming the relation**

- antipattern: "The valve stuck. Pressure rose. The line burst."
- pattern: "Because the valve stuck, pressure rose until the line burst."
- observe: the antipattern leaves the relation between the events for the audience to supply; the pattern states it as cause.
- shared: the same three events, in the same order.
- contrast: side-by-side clauses against ranked ones.
- look for: what side-by-side sentences leave for the audience to connect, if anything.

Where pace matters more than cause, the short sentences are right.

The ratio is a register dial. All-paratactic reads as wire copy. All-hypotactic reads as a legal opinion. Where the ratio pulls against the content, such as a condolence note in wire-copy bursts, ask whether that pull is chosen.

## The cascade

Length sets a beat. When sentences share one length, the ear learns the count and stops listening, however good the words. A long sentence after short ones releases the count; a short one after a long one lands. Vary length where the content has a build or a turn to carry.

**Contrast: varied length**

- antipattern: "The ferry left at six. The sea was calm. Gulls followed the wake. The passengers stood at the rail. The island came into view. The engines slowed down."
- pattern: "The ferry left at six. The sea was calm. Gulls followed the wake, and the passengers stood at the rail while the island came into view. The engines slowed down."
- observe: the middle three sentences join, so the short ones on either side land by contrast.
- shared: every fact, and nearly every word.
- contrast: sentence length alone.
- look for: three or more sentences in a row within two words of each other in length.

## Questions

- Where, if anywhere, does sentence length cluster — three or more sentences in a row of nearly identical length — and is the evenness a refrain, a litany, or a run of steps the form wants?
- For each long sentence, which clause does it end on, and what does that clause carry?
- For each short sentence after a long one, what work, if any, is it doing, such as closure, reversal, accent, or punchline?
- What does each fragment do: continue the sentence before it, set apart for accent, carry the form's own grammar as a line of verse or a list of images does, or stand as a slip?
- For each dash, what work does it do, such as an interrupt or a pivot, that a comma would not?
- Does the parataxis/hypotaxis ratio match the register the content asks for, or does the sentence-grammar style argue against the situation?
- Read the passage aloud at speaking pace. Where, if anywhere, does the breath misalign with the punctuation, and what does the misalignment reveal?

## Diagnostics in practice

When auditing a draft:

1. **Read aloud at speaking pace.** Where do you run out of breath? Where do you take a breath the punctuation did not mark? A mismatch between breath and punctuation points to pacing worth checking.
2. **Mark the sentence-length sequence.** Write the word count of each sentence. Where three sit nearly level, ask whether the form wants that evenness; where it does not, change one.
3. **Audit the long sentences.** Which clause does each end on? Where a long sentence trails into a qualifier the piece did not mean to stress, rewrite.
4. **Audit short sentences after long ones.** What does each short one do? Where it does no work, cut or merge.
5. **Audit fragments.** Does each read as the continuation of the sentence before it? Where one does not, and the form does not use fragments this way, fix it.
6. **Audit one-sentence paragraphs.** A standalone sentence is louder than its surroundings. Which of them carries the weight that loudness claims? Fold the others into a neighbor.
7. **Count dashes.** How many does the piece hold, and does each interrupt or pivot?
8. **Test the parataxis/hypotaxis ratio.** Which ratio does the content and its audience call for, and where does the draft depart from it?
