# Fit

Fits when: the form of the piece is still open, or its shape and its audience's purpose may pull apart

Assess whether the prose's form serves its communicative purpose -- does the shape match the goal?

## Diagnostic Question

Ask: Does the form match the function? Where, if anywhere, does the shape work against the intent?
For each section, ask: what shape does it take, such as argumentative, expository, evocative, or dense, and what shape does its purpose call for? What would happen to the purpose if the form changed?

## Instructions

1. **Name the communicative purpose** -- What must this prose accomplish? Inform, persuade, explore, provoke, instruct, narrate, propose, bind, specify, honor? Purpose is not genre -- a proposal can inform, a narrative can persuade. Identify the verb, or the two verbs, not the container.

2. **Identify the form currently in use** -- What organizational and rhetorical shape has the writer chosen? Expository (here is what happened), argumentative (here is why you should believe X), exploratory (here is what we don't know), instructional (here is how to do X), evocative (here is what it felt like), binding (here is what each party must do). Name it without judgment.

3. **Test form-function alignment** -- Does the form serve the purpose? An expository form on persuasive content leaves the audience informed but unmoved. An argumentative form on exploratory content forecloses the inquiry it claims to open. An evocative form on instructional content leaves the audience stirred but unable to act. Where form and function diverge, the prose works against itself.

4. **Check for mixed purposes** -- Prose often serves multiple purposes simultaneously. This is not a problem unless the purposes conflict. A proposal that both instructs and persuades is coherent. A report that both informs and evokes may split its audience's attention. Name the primary purpose and identify where secondary purposes support or undermine it.

5. **Assess necessity** -- Why must this prose exist? What would be lost if it were not written? If the writer cannot answer, the form-function question is premature -- the piece has not yet found its reason. Necessity precedes form: you cannot choose the right shape for a piece that has no purpose.

6. **Recommend form adjustments** -- When form and function diverge, propose specific structural changes. Do not rewrite -- name what would change and why.

## Questions

A short interrogation to run on a passage when form-function alignment is in doubt. The questions name what to inspect. They don't prescribe what to conclude.

- What verb, or pair of verbs, names what this prose is actually trying to do — inform, persuade, instruct, explore, evoke, narrate, bind, honor?
- Look at the shape on the page, separately from what I meant. Where does the architecture lean — argumentative, expository, exploratory, instructional, evocative, binding?
- Where do those two answers diverge?
- If someone in the audience stopped where I stopped writing, would they be in the state I needed them in — informed, moved, ready to act, drawn into the question, witnessed, bound?
- Where, if anywhere, is the shape doing the opposite of what the content invites? An argumentative form on an open question closes inquiry the content meant to keep alive. An expository form on persuasive content leaves the audience informed but unmoved. An evocative form on instructional content stirs the audience and leaves them unable to act on Tuesday.
- What would actually change if I switched form? If I am reluctant to switch, is the reluctance about the audience, or about me?

## Quality Criteria

When the form-function alignment is sound:

- [ ] The communicative purpose is nameable in a verb or two — inform, persuade, instruct, explore, evoke, narrate, bind, honor, or another verb that fits.
- [ ] The shape of the writing matches that purpose. Argumentative content lives in argumentative shape; open questions stay in exploratory shape; persuasive content lives in a shape that builds toward decision; instructional content lives in a shape the audience can act from.
- [ ] Someone in the audience who stops mid-passage is left in the state the purpose required — informed, moved, ready to act, drawn in, witnessed.
- [ ] No section is doing the opposite of what its content invites.
- [ ] The current form survives the question "would changing this serve the audience better?" — and the honest answer is no.
