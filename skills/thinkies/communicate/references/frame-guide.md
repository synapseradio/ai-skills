# Compose from a Blank Page

Fits when: the work starts from a blank page, with no draft and no outline

Read this when the user starts from nothing: no draft, no outline, often only a felt need. Establish four things before any structural decision: necessity, position, tradition, audience. A piece built before these are known inherits guesses in all four.

## Necessity

Ask why this must be written. Writing driven by necessity produces different work than writing driven by instruction.

- What would be lost if this were not written? When the answer says nothing, the piece may not need to exist yet; saying so helps more than fabricating a reason.
- Whose experience does this preserve? A record of voices no other account keeps, or a story that has to exist even where someone would silence it, has its necessity in plain view.
- What does this piece insist upon? Necessity can be existential, political, or personal, and it must be nameable.
- Does the writer already know what they want to say, or does a felt need still lack a form? Transmission calls for framing now. Discovery calls for an exploratory draft first; revisit these questions after it reveals what the piece wants to say.

When the user cannot articulate necessity, treat that as information rather than failure. Ask what event, conversation, or realization made this feel urgent. Necessity needs no drama: an ordinary experience examined closely is reason enough.

## Position

Writing comes from somewhere, and that somewhere decides what the piece can claim. [voice](./voice.md) holds three questions to ask before composing: what relationship the writer has to this subject, what they can legitimately claim from it, and what they cannot see from where they stand.

Treat the answers as structural decisions. Position picks the stance (witness, expert, participant), the available evidence (lived experience, research, testimony), and the fitting voice. A choice such as which language to write in commits the work, not only its styling. Where does position show in the piece: in what it claims and how, or in a paragraph that announces it ("As a [identity], I acknowledge...")?

## Tradition

Identify the rhetorical tradition the piece will operate in before choosing its structure. Ask the user which tradition the piece works in, what tradition the audience expects, and what conventions the genre imposes over both; never infer the tradition from the language anyone speaks.

When the writer's tradition and the audience's differ, flag it and put one question to the user: adapt to the audience's tradition, or keep the writer's and bridge explicitly? [across-languages](./across-languages.md) holds tradition patterns and what each adaptation costs.

## Audience

Map what the audience already knows ([calibrate](./calibrate.md) helps), what conventions they expect, and their relationship to the subject: peers, novices, skeptics, allies, decision-makers. Then name what the writer wants them to do, feel, or understand.

When that purpose cannot land with this audience, say so. Either the purpose or the audience shifts, and the user decides which.
