# excavate-assumptions: training the office parrot as receptionist

- skill: excavate-assumptions, from commit 0c9258f
- subject: Our plan to train the office parrot as the receptionist: it already says "hello" and "one moment please", so we will teach it the three most common visitor questions and save a salary.
- kind: single run

## Prompt

/excavate-assumptions Our plan to train the office parrot as the receptionist: it already says "hello" and "one moment please", so we will teach it the three most common visitor questions and save a salary.

## Run

### 1. Every assumption in the framing

The plan reads: the parrot says "hello" and "one moment please" now, so we teach it the three most common visitor questions and save a salary. Pulled apart, it holds these assumptions.

1. A1. The parrot's two phrases are a skill to build on, so adding three more is the same kind of work.
2. A2. The parrot says "hello" and "one moment please" at fitting moments, not at random.
3. A3. A parrot can be taught to ask questions and use them in context, not only repeat sounds it has heard.
4. A4. The three most common visitor questions are known, and they are the same three each week.
5. A5. Visitors mostly ask those three questions, so covering them covers most arrivals.
6. A6. The visitor's questions are the whole job. The parrot only has to talk; a receptionist's work is speaking.
7. A7. Asking a question is enough. The parrot does not have to understand the answer or act on it.
8. A8. The parrot will do this reliably, every day, for years, in front of strangers.
9. A9. Visitors will accept a parrot at the desk and stay for the interaction.
10. A10. The parrot is the office's to assign: nobody objects, and the parrot's own welfare allows a working role.
11. A11. A salary is what the reception role costs, so removing the person removes the cost.
12. A12. Nothing else the receptionist does has value. The salary buys only the talking.
13. A13. The parrot's cost is near zero.
14. A14. The office needs a receptionist function, and the parrot is the option to test, not the answer to "do we need one".

### 2. Dig at each level

**Semantic: what must be true for this to be meaningful**

- "Receptionist" must mean "the thing that greets and routes visitors". If it means a person who decides, admits, and escalates, "train the parrot as the receptionist" does not describe a possible outcome.
- "Say" must mean the same for the parrot and for a person. A parrot producing the sounds "one moment please" is a sound played back. Whether the sound counts as saying it, in the sense of committing to the visitor that a moment is what they will wait, is exactly where the two meanings split.
- "Teach the question" must mean the parrot produces the question at the right cue. If it means the parrot understands the question, that is a different, larger claim.
- "Most common" must refer to a count someone took. If nobody counted, "most common" is a felt impression and the plan's central number, three, has nothing under it.

**Pragmatic: what must be true for this to be worth saying**

- The plan is worth stating only if the salary is a cost the office wants to remove. If the receptionist also covers phones, deliveries, post, badge issuing, and answering the door, the salary buys a bundle and the plan compares a parrot against one item of it.
- The plan is worth acting on only if the saving survives the parrot's costs: food, a cage or perch at the desk, cleaning, vet care, noise, and someone to supervise. A parrot is a long-lived animal, so these run for decades, not months.
- The plan is worth saying only if someone considered a cheaper baseline: a doorbell, a sign, a tablet check-in, or a screen with the three answers on it. Any of those also removes the salary and none has to be trained.
- The speaker presumably wants the office to look competent and welcoming. The plan is only worth doing if a parrot at the desk serves that, and it may serve it in the opposite direction.

**Logical: what must hold for the reasoning to work**

- The argument runs: the parrot can already say two phrases, so it can say five. This is an induction from two cases to a third-plus, and it holds only if the two existing phrases are the same kind of behavior as the new ones.
- "Hello" and "one moment please" are both cue-free courtesies. They work at nearly any moment: a greeting fits any arrival, and a wait phrase fits nearly any pause. A question fits only its own cue, and the answer to it varies. The step from courtesy to question crosses a boundary the argument treats as flat.
- The plan needs both parts to be true, a question asked and the answer heard, for the visitor to be helped. The plan only covers asking. The logic from "asks three questions" to "receives visitors" skips what happens after the visitor answers.
- The saving conclusion needs the parrot to replace the person. It only holds if nobody is needed for the replies the parrot cannot make. The moment one person is still needed to catch the parrot's failures, the salary is not saved, it is redistributed.

**Framework: what worldview this assumes**

- Reception is a set of scripted exchanges. A script is a complete description of the job, so anything that recites a script can do it.
- Labor is a cost to be minimized. The receptionist is a line in a budget, not a person who notices a visitor is unwell, lost, or unwelcome.
- An animal is an instrument the office may put to work, with its capacity for the role judged by output alone.
- The office is a place where visits are routine and low-stakes. If a visit is a client signing a contract, a candidate interviewing, or an emergency, the desk carries more than a greeting.
- The visitor is a passive recipient of the office's presentation, willing to be amused. In a worldview where the visitor's first impression sets the terms of the relationship, the parrot is a bet on that relationship.

### 4. Focus the attention

Both high on importance and low on certainty: A1, A3, A5, A6, A7, A8, A11, A12. These are the ones to spend effort on. They fall into three clusters.

- **Can the parrot do it?** A1, A3, A8. Does the parrot ask a question at its cue and keep doing so?
- **Is the job only the talking?** A5, A6, A7, A12. Do three questions cover most visitors, and is the reply to their answer, plus everything else the person does, unneeded?
- **Is the saving real?** A11 and A13. Does removing the salary cost less than the parrot's upkeep and supervision?

A2, A4, A9, A10, and A13 matter less to the outcome, or are cheap to settle later, and stay on the list without a test now. A14 is medium on both and is the assumption to confirm in one sentence before anything else. If the office needs no reception function, the plan is moot.

The cluster with the most leverage is the second. If A6 and A12 fail, the plan fails whatever the parrot can do, since the job then contains work the parrot cannot do. That cluster is tested first.

### 5. Test each focused assumption

Each gets two questions: what evidence would disprove it, and what follows if the opposite were true.

**A1. Two phrases extend to five**

- Disproved by: teaching the parrot one question and watching whether it asks at the visitor's arrival, or plays it at random like any other sound it has picked up. One week of trying is enough to see.
- If the opposite were true: the two current phrases are near-random utterances that happen to suit a desk. The plan then adds noise. Most of the parrot's "reception" so far is coincidence.

**A3. A parrot can use questions in context, not only repeat**

- Disproved by: a trial in which the cue (a person walking in) is held fixed and the question is heard at the cue on most arrivals and at few other times. If the rate at the cue is no higher than the rate elsewhere, the parrot repeats but does not use.
- If the opposite were true: the parrot is a recording with a beak. It can be entertaining and cannot be a receptionist, and the plan becomes a question about mascots.

**A5. Three questions cover most visits**

- Disproved by: a two-week tally at the desk of every question asked, sorted by frequency. If the top three are under half of the total, the parrot handles a minority.
- If the opposite were true: the tail of questions is where visitors are actually stuck, and the parrot turns every visitor with an unusual need into a person waiting for a human anyway.

**A6. The job is talking**

- Disproved by: asking the current receptionist for a day's log of duties, and marking each duty the parrot could do with a recited phrase. Any duty that needs a decision, a signature, a key, a phone, or a judgment about a stranger is a duty outside the plan.
- If the opposite were true: reception is mostly deciding and acting, and the talking is what remains once they are done. Replacing the talking replaces the smallest part.

**A7. Asking is enough**

- Disproved by: writing out the exchange for each of the three questions, from the visitor's first word to the visitor's next step. If any exchange ends with the visitor needing an answer, a direction, or a person, the exchange is unfinished.
- If the opposite were true: the visitor answers the parrot and receives nothing. The parrot has taken the first turn of a conversation it cannot continue, and each visitor has had a small experience of being ignored.

**A8. Reliable, daily, for years**

- Disproved by: a supervised month at the desk. A single day with a stranger who is loud, or a day the parrot is silent, disproves reliability. Watch for the other failure too: an unsupervised parrot that says "one moment please" to the courier at midnight.
- If the opposite were true: the desk works on good days. A person is needed as backup on the rest, and the backup is the salary.

**A11. Removing the person removes the cost**

- Disproved by: a costed comparison of the salary against parrot food, housing at the desk, vet care, cleaning, breakage, noise complaints, and the hours someone spends supervising or covering. If they come close to the salary, or a partial hire is still needed, the saving does not exist.
- If the opposite were true: the office swaps a visible, budgeted cost for several hidden ones, spread over people who never agreed to take them on.

**A12. Nothing else the receptionist does matters**

- Disproved by: the same duty log as A6, together with a question to the receptionist: what would go wrong the day you were out and nobody covered? Any answer with a consequence is a duty with value.
- If the opposite were true: the salary buys judgment. The office loses the person who notices things and gains a parrot that does not.

### Result

The plan's weight rests on eight assumptions that are important and unsupported, in three clusters: the parrot's capacity, the job's real size, and the saving. Four of the eight (A6, A7, A11, A12) are about the job and the cost, and can be tested in a week with paper and a stopwatch, before any training. The cheapest first step is the duty log and the visitor-question tally, because if they show reception is more than three recited questions, the parrot is not a candidate whatever it can learn.
