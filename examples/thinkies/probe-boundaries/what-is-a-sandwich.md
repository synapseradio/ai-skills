# probe-boundaries: what is a sandwich

- skill: probe-boundaries, from commit 0c9258f
- subject: "A sandwich is anything between two pieces of bread."
- kind: single run

## Prompt

/probe-boundaries A sandwich is anything between two pieces of bread.

## Run

The claim under test: "A sandwich is anything between two pieces of bread." It is a definition, so it asserts two things at once. Anything between two pieces of bread is a sandwich (sufficient), and nothing else is a sandwich (necessary). The probe checks both directions.

### 1. Load-bearing variables

Five words carry the claim. If any one bends, the definition bends with it.

- **"anything"**: what may sit in the middle.
- **"between"**: the spatial relation of the middle to the bread.
- **"two"**: the count of bread units.
- **"pieces"**: bread as discrete, separate units.
- **"bread"**: the carrier material.

"Is" is a sixth, carried into step 5, because it sets the kind of claim: identity rather than resemblance.

### 2. Push to extremes

**"anything", maximized.** The filling outweighs the bread by a factor of a thousand: a whole roast pig between two slices, the slices lost against it. The claim still says sandwich. The pull toward "no" here is about proportion, and proportion appears nowhere in the claim.

**"anything", minimized.** The filling is a smear of butter, then a fingerprint of oil, then one grain of salt. The claim says sandwich at every step, and no step marks where the answer should change. The only edge the claim offers is the edge of nothing, which step 3 takes up.

**"between", maximized in distance.** Two slices sit ten meters apart with a person standing between them. The claim, read literally, says the person is a sandwich filling. "Between" needs contact or containment to mean anything a cook would recognize, and the claim never says so.

**"between", minimized to a hinge.** The bread touches only along one edge and the filling sits in the fold. This is the hot dog bun. The claim's "two pieces" no longer describes what is on the plate.

**"two", maximized.** A stack of forty slices, filling between each pair. Every adjacent pair satisfies the claim, so the stack holds thirty-nine sandwiches, or one. The claim counts pieces of bread and gives no way to count sandwiches. The club sandwich (three slices) is the mild version of the same problem.

**"two", minimized.** One slice with filling on top, the open-faced sandwich. The claim excludes it, though ordinary usage calls it a sandwich.

**"pieces", maximized in size.** Two loaves the length of a room with a person-sized filling between. The claim allows it. Nothing in the word "pieces" limits scale, and a sandwich in ordinary use is something one person holds.

**"pieces", minimized in size.** Two crumbs with a fleck of jam between. The claim says sandwich. At this scale nobody could eat it as a unit or see the structure.

**"bread", stretched.** A tortilla, a pita, injera, a bun, a bagel, two crackers, two cookies with ice cream between. Where "bread" ends is not fixed. The ice cream sandwich fails the claim on its material and satisfies the shape, and people call it a sandwich anyway.

### 3. Test absence

- **No filling.** Two slices pressed together. The claim survives only by a technicality: air is "anything", and so is nothing. A definition that admits nothing as its middle has stopped constraining the middle.
- **No bread.** Ham alone. The claim correctly says no. This is the one absence the claim handles.
- **No "between".** Two slices on a plate, ham beside them, assembled by nobody. The claim says no, and so does ordinary usage, until the diner puts the pieces together. The line is drawn by act (assembly), not by the presence of ingredients.
- **No "two".** One slice with the filling on it. Discussed above as the open-faced case.
- **No "pieces".** A single roll, sliced through and filled, with the cut left uncut on one side. The material and geometry are those of a sandwich, and the word "pieces" is doing the excluding.

### 4. Change kind, not just degree

Here the scenario changes category, not amount.

- **From food to object.** Two slices with a pencil between them. By the claim, a sandwich. By use, a craft project. The claim never requires that the middle be edible, and that requirement seems to be what a cook silently supplies.
- **From object to structure.** Laminated glass is one pane, a plastic film, a second pane. A sandwich board is a sign hung between two panels. A seat on a bench between two strangers is "sandwiched". The word is used for the shape (something held between two of something else) with bread nowhere in it. The shape is older than the food, or at least independent of it.
- **From noun to verb.** To sandwich a task between two meetings. Here the word takes only the "between" and drops the bread. This suggests "between" is the load-bearing part of the meaning and "bread" is the prototype that filled the slot first.
- **From solid to liquid.** Soup between two bread bowls, or a sauce pooled between slices. The claim admits it. A diner might not, because it cannot be lifted and eaten by hand.
- **From meal to tool.** Two slices with something between them, used to prop a wobbly table leg. Ingredients and geometry match. The function does not, and no one would call it a sandwich.
- **From a shared meaning to a stated rule.** A tax code, a lease, or a menu at a restaurant that charges differently for "sandwiches". Here the definition must decide a hot dog, a burrito, and an open-faced slice ahead of time. The claim decides none of them.

### 5. Stress the framing itself

Key words, each with substitutes.

| Word | Substitutes | What the substitution shows |
|---|---|---|
| "is" | "counts as", "is usually called", "is treated as, for this menu" | "Is" claims a fixed identity. The substitutes allow a category defined by resemblance or by context, and the hot dog dispute lives there. |
| "anything" | "any edible thing", "any food that holds together" | The original admits pencils and people. The substitutes bring in an unstated requirement of edibility. |
| "between" | "enclosed by", "held by", "sandwiched by", "supported by" | Each verb decides a different case: "enclosed" excludes the hot dog bun, "held" admits it, "supported" admits the open-faced slice. |
| "two" | "at least two", "a top and a bottom", "a carrier" | "Two" was chosen for the ordinary case. "A top and a bottom" admits the club sandwich. "A carrier" admits the open-faced and the hinged. |
| "pieces" | "portions", "layers", "slices", "halves" | "Halves" implies one thing cut in two, which admits the bun. "Layers" admits the stack. |
| "bread" | "bread-like", "a baked starch", "a carrier", "the outer part" | "Bread" keeps out the cookie and the cracker. "A carrier" lets them in, and also lets in a lettuce leaf. |

The framing constrains thinking in two ways. It treats the question as a membership test with a single yes or no, and it treats the definition as belonging to the object rather than to whoever is using the word. A cook, a menu writer, and a tax code each need a different edge, and the claim gives them all the same one.

### 6. Examine the failures

The claim fails at the boundaries in two opposite directions.

**Too broad (fails the sufficient half).** The pencil between two slices, the person between two distant slices, the empty pair of slices, the forty-slice stack counted as one, the two-crumb version. In each, the bread and the geometry are as the claim wants, and something everyone sees is missing: an edible filling, a middle in contact with the bread, a unit a person can pick up and eat. The claim describes geometry and material only, and the word "sandwich" also names a purpose (a handheld meal), which the claim omits.

**Too narrow (fails the necessary half).** The open-faced slice, the hot dog bun, the ice cream sandwich, the tortilla wrap, the cookie sandwich. In each, either the count, the material, or the joint differs from the claim, and ordinary usage still says sandwich (or argues over it). The claim treats "two pieces of bread" as required when it is only the prototype.

**The single cause.** The claim fixes three things (filling, position, bread) that are, in use, held together by a fourth thing that it never states: a middle enclosed by a carrier, eaten as one unit. Once that is named, both directions of failure make sense. The pencil lacks the edible middle. The ice cream sandwich keeps the enclosed middle and the eaten unit while changing the carrier.

**What survived.** Two pieces held around a middle, with the middle's ingredient unrestricted, held up under every push to an extreme in the "anything" direction, apart from edibility. The structure (a middle held between two carriers) also held across the change of kind in step 4, where it carried the word into glass, signs, benches, and meetings.

### 7. Revise the scope

**Revised claim.** A sandwich is an edible filling held between two pieces of a carrier, eaten as one handheld unit. Bread is the prototype carrier and not a requirement. Open-faced and hinged forms (a single slice with topping, a split bun) are borderline members that ordinary usage often admits.

**Limits on the revised claim.**

1. It is a description of everyday usage. It does not settle cases such as the hot dog or the burrito, because ordinary usage does not settle them either.
2. Where the word decides a price, a tax, or a contract, a stated rule replaces the description. The rule names the disputed cases outright.
3. It keeps the structural sense ("between two of something") as an extension of the word and does not treat the structural sense as its core.

**Verdicts under both versions.**

| Case | Original claim | Revised claim |
|---|---|---|
| Ham and cheese between two slices | sandwich | sandwich |
| Roast pig between two slices | sandwich | sandwich, at the edge of "handheld" |
| Pencil between two slices | sandwich | not a sandwich (not edible) |
| Two slices ten meters apart, person between | sandwich | not a sandwich (not held) |
| Two slices, nothing between | sandwich (vacuous) | not a sandwich (no filling) |
| Forty-slice stack | thirty-nine sandwiches or one | one stacked sandwich |
| Open-faced slice | not a sandwich | borderline, usually admitted |
| Hot dog in a split bun | not a sandwich | borderline, usually admitted |
| Ice cream between two cookies | not a sandwich | sandwich (carrier stretched) |
| Ham beside two slices, unassembled | not a sandwich | not a sandwich |

**Not tested in this run.** How different food traditions draw the line, and whether a single stated rule could satisfy a lease, a menu, and a dinner conversation at once. Both would change the limits above and neither was probed.
