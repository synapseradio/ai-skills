# trace-logical-justifications: never throw away a working pen

- skill: trace-logical-justifications, from commit 0c9258f
- subject: "We must never throw away a working pen."
- kind: single run

## Prompt

/trace-logical-justifications We must never throw away a working pen.

## Run

The subject is a single sentence, so the chain below is reconstructed: each claim is one the sentence needs in order to hold, stated as its speaker would have to state it.

### 1. The claim chain

- **C1 (conclusion).** We must never throw away a working pen.
- **C2.** A working pen still does the job a pen is for: it writes.
- **C3.** Discarding a thing that still does its job is waste.
- **C4.** Waste is wrong.
- **C5.** Wrong acts are to be avoided without exception (this is what turns "should" into "must never").
- **C6.** A discarded pen cannot be recovered, and it might be needed later.
- **C7.** A throwaway pen is plastic, and plastic in a landfill does lasting harm.
- **C8.** Nothing about a pen's situation can make discarding it the better choice.
- **C9.** The obligation binds everyone ("we"), not only this speaker.

The chain's shape: C2 feeds C3, C3 and C4 feed C1, and C5 upgrades C1 from "should" to "must never". C6 and C7 are offered as extra support for C3 and C4. C8 and C9 are what the words "never" and "we" quietly require.

### 2. What supports each claim

| Claim | Warrant type | Support |
|---|---|---|
| C2 | Definition | "Working" means able to write, and writing is what a pen is for. |
| C3 | Another claim, plus a definition | Depends on "waste" meaning "discarding something that still serves its purpose". If so, C3 follows from C2 by definition. |
| C4 | Nothing visible | No reason is given for why waste is wrong. Candidate sources: frugality, environmental ethics, respect for the labour that made the pen. The sentence names none. |
| C5 | Nothing visible | Moves from "wrong" to "wrong without exception". No warrant given. |
| C6 | Observation, partly | Irreversibility is true of most discarded objects (a bin is collected). "Might be needed later" is a prediction with no measurement behind it. |
| C7 | Observation, borrowed | Depends on a fact about pen materials and disposal that the speaker has not measured or cited. The fact is checkable; it is not on the page. |
| C8 | Nothing visible | Universal claim about all pens in all situations. Nothing is offered. |
| C9 | Nothing visible | Nothing says why the obligation extends past the speaker. |
| C1 | Another claim | Follows from C3, C4, C5, and C8 taken together. |

### 3. Following the chains to bedrock

- **C1 -> C3 -> C2 -> definition of "working" and "pen".** This chain ends at bedrock of the definitional kind. Nothing else in the argument does.
- **C1 -> C3 -> C4 -> absence.** The step from "this is waste" to "waste is wrong" has no warrant. This is the moral premise the whole argument turns on.
- **C1 -> C5 -> absence.** "Never" is unsupported.
- **C1 -> C8 -> absence.** No warrant that pens are exempt from every counterexample.
- **C1 -> C6 -> half observation, half absence.** Irreversibility is a matter of observation (a discarded pen is gone once collected). "Might be needed later" ends in a guess.
- **C1 -> C7 -> unmeasured observation.** This chain could reach bedrock (a disposal figure or a material fact), but the speaker has not gone there.
- **C1 -> C9 -> absence.**

Only one chain reaches definitional bedrock. Only C6's first half and C7 could reach observation bedrock, and neither has yet.

### 4. Floating claims

Four claims float, that is, their chains end in absence:

1. **C4. Waste is wrong.** The argument's moral premise.
2. **C5. Wrong acts are to be avoided without exception.** The premise that supplies "never".
3. **C8. No situation makes discarding a working pen better.** The universal premise.
4. **C9. The obligation binds everyone.** The scope premise.

Two more are partly floating:

1. **C6b. The pen might be needed later.** Prediction with no evidence.
2. **C7. Plastic disposal does lasting harm.** Could be grounded; is not.

Testing C8 with cases shows why it matters. A pen that is technically working but leaks over the pocket it sits in. A person who owns four hundred pens and no drawer left to keep them in. A pen found on a train, belonging to someone else. A pen in a bag going through a place where pens are prohibited. Each is a working pen, and in each the sentence's "never" forces an answer that few would accept. A working pen appears to be the only fact the speaker weighed; the pen's situation was not.

### 5. Chain integrity

- **Are bedrock warrants appropriate for the claims they support?** The one definitional bedrock (C2, that a working pen writes) is appropriate for what it supports, and is nearly trivially true. It supports only "the pen is still useful". It cannot reach "must never discard", because a definition of "working" cannot settle whether usefulness obliges keeping.
- **Are the floating assumptions plausible?** C4 is plausible as a default (most people dislike waste) and needs only a stated source to become defensible. C7 is plausible and checkable. C6b is plausible but weak: for a cheap, common object, "I might need it later" is the reasoning behind most clutter. C5, C8, and C9 are the implausible ones. C5 and C8 together assert that no exception exists, and a single counterexample from the cases above breaks them. C9 asserts a shared duty over a private possession, with no reason offered.
- **Does the overall structure hold?** The inference from C2, C3, and C4 to a soft version of the conclusion holds: a working pen is a thing that still serves its purpose, and discarding it is usually waste that a person has some reason to avoid. The full conclusion does not hold. Its strength ("must never", "we") comes entirely from floating claims. The argument is valid for "we should usually be reluctant to throw away a working pen" and unsupported for the sentence as written.

**Where the chain can be repaired.** Stating a source for C4 (for instance, that disposal costs the environment something measurable) would give C4 and C7 an observation to stand on. Replacing "never" with "unless" and a named list of exceptions would remove C5 and C8 from the chain. Replacing "we" with "I" would remove C9.
