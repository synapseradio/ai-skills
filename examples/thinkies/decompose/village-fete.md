**Subject:** Running a village fête: the goal of carrying out, from first commitment to last tidy-up, a one-day outdoor community event held in a village.

1. What is running a village fête for, and where does it end: what belongs inside the work and what does not?

   The purpose is unknown. A fête usually serves one or more of three ends: raising money for a cause, bringing the village together, or keeping a tradition alive. Those ends pull in different directions (a money fête prices stalls high and charges entry; a gathering fête keeps everything cheap so nobody is priced out), and the request does not choose. Every cut below is provisional until someone names the purpose.

   The boundary, held on the reading that the work runs from the decision to hold the fête until the field is clear and the money is counted and banked. Inside: getting permission, money, people, stock, and equipment; the day itself; clearing up; the accounts. Outside, provisionally: what the raised money is spent on afterwards, the village's other events, and running the organizing committee as a standing body beyond this one fête.

2. Which axis fits for cutting the goal of running a village fête at its top level, and which relations along that axis?

   Means-to. "Run a village fête" is a goal, so the cut asks what must be achieved for the goal to be achieved. Part-of would cut the event itself into stalls, field, and tent, and would leave out everything that happens before and after the day.

   The relations selected are subgoals and constraints. Cases are left out, since no branching condition shows up at this level (weather is a case, and belongs to a lower level). Along subgoals, each part is something that must be true by the time the gates open or the last van leaves.

3. Where are the natural joints in the goal of running a village fête?

   Six parts, each tested against the four marks of a joint (small interface, independent reasons to change, abrupt change in properties, one function of the whole):

   - **Permission and safety**: the landowner's or council's consent, public liability insurance, licences for alcohol, music, and food, and a plan for first aid and emergencies. Its interface with the rest is a short list of conditions (capacity, closing hours, what may be sold). It changes for legal reasons only.
   - **Money**: a budget, a float, a way to take payments, and the count at the end. Its interface is spending limits going out and takings coming in. It changes with the purpose and the sponsors.
   - **People**: the organizing committee, stallholders, helpers, and their rota. Its interface is a roster of who is where and when. It changes with who volunteers.
   - **Programme**: what the fête offers, meaning stalls, games, performances, food, and the raffle. Its interface is a list of things needing space, power, and stock. It changes with taste and tradition.
   - **Site and supplies**: the field, marquees, tables, power, water, toilets, parking, signs, and the stock and prizes that arrive. Its interface is a plan showing what stands where. It changes with the weather and what can be borrowed.
   - **Promotion**: posters, the village notice, social media, and word of mouth. Its interface is a date, a place, and a reason to come. It changes with the season and audience.

   Properties change abruptly at each line: permission is about law, money about numbers, people about goodwill, programme about appeal, site about physical things, promotion about attention. Time runs through all six and is not a seventh part; it is the ordering of their milestones.

4. Do the parts cut from running a village fête cover it exactly, with no gap and no overlap?

   Checked against the first pass, and it found two gaps and two overlaps.

   - Gap: the day itself. Six parts prepare for it, and none holds the running of the day (opening, handling problems, closing). Added as a seventh part, **The day**, whose interface is the roster and the site plan.
   - Gap: clearing up and closing out (litter, returning borrowed equipment, thank-yous, banking, the accounts). Added as an eighth part, **Afterwards**.
   - Overlap: buying stock and prizes sat in both Money and Site and supplies. Moved wholly to Site and supplies; Money holds only the budget line and the payment.
   - Overlap: recruiting stallholders sat in both People and Programme. Moved to People (who takes part); Programme holds only what each stall offers.

   After the moves, the eight parts cover the goal exactly, on the provisional reading of its boundary from question 1: Permission and safety, Money, People, Programme, Site and supplies, Promotion, The day, Afterwards.

5. Which parts of running a village fête need another pass?

   Three of the eight are still too complex to act on: Programme, Site and supplies, and The day. The other five each end in actions one person can take this week (apply for the licence, open a bank account, post the rota, print the posters, return the tables). The second pass runs the same steps on each of the three, naming the axis at every level.

   **Programme, second pass. Axis: part-of, for things (the fête is a set of activities).** Relation: members. Cut by kind of activity, which is where properties change abruptly (a cake stall needs a table and a tin, a band needs a stage and power): **stalls** (cakes, plants, books, bric-à-brac), **games** (coconut shy, welly throw, tombola), **performances** (band, dancers, dog show), **food and drink** (tea tent, barbecue, bar), and the **raffle**. They overlap nowhere, since each activity is exactly one kind. They leave a gap that the raffle nearly hides: things nobody thought of yet. That gap is the reason the programme's owner keeps an "other" line open until a set date.

   **Site and supplies, second pass. Axis: part-of, for places.** Relation: places. Cut by zone, since each zone has its own needs: **arrival** (parking, gate, signs), **the field** (stall pitches, games, performance area), **the tent** (tea, seating, shelter), **services** (toilets, water, power, first aid point, bins), and **stock in and out** (a delivery and collection route across all zones). "Stock in and out" is a flow across the zones and overlaps them, so it is cut as a thing handled at two moments instead, in Site and supplies before the day and in Afterwards on the day after. The overlap is gone.

   **The day, second pass. Axis: means-to, for tasks, with the relation phases.** Relation: phases in time, since the day's tasks differ by when they fall: **setup** (early morning: pitches marked, tables out, floats issued), **opening** (the gate, the welcome), **the middle** (running the programme, handling breakdowns, weather, lost children), and **closing** (last sales, raffle draw, first sweep). A fifth cut, **weather**, is a case that cuts across all four phases. Case relations fit here at last, and only here: the plan for rain differs from the plan for sun in setup, the middle, and closing. Weather is therefore one part with two branches (dry, wet) that each hold a changed version of the phases, and is not a fifth phase.

   Part-of does not chain into means-to: the Programme's list of kinds of activity is a part-of cut and does not become a list of tasks. Each part's own tasks (book the band, collect the cakes) belong to a fresh means-to pass on that part if it ever needs one.

6. Where does cutting stop for each part of running a village fête?

   Cutting stops at a part one person can act on or check directly.

   - **Stops at the first pass:** Permission and safety, Money, People, Promotion, Afterwards. Each is one owner's job with a visible finish (the licence in hand, the float counted, the rota full, the posters up, the field clear and banked).
   - **Stops at the second pass:** the five Programme kinds, the five site zones, and the four day phases plus the weather branches. Each is small enough to hand to a named person, and each can be checked by looking (the tea tent has urn, cups, and a washing-up bowl; the setup phase is done when the last pitch has its table).
   - **Stops before a third pass:** cutting a stall kind into its individual stalls, or a zone into its objects, grows the interfaces more than it shrinks the parts. Forty stalls share the same table, the same pitch rules, and the same float, so forty separate parts would mean forty near-identical interfaces to keep in step.

   Open on all of it: the purpose. If the fête exists to raise money, Money and Promotion grow (pricing, sponsorship, ticketing) and may need their own second pass. If it exists to gather the village, People and Programme grow and Money shrinks to the float. The eight-part cut holds on either reading, and the depth of each part shifts.
