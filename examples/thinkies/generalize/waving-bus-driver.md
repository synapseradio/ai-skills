1. Which particulars of the route 12 driver's case (the number 12, the city, the number of daily riders, the vehicle, the wave itself) can be dropped without changing the case's shape, and which must stay?

   Droppable, checked one at a time:

   - The number 12 and the city. The parts stay the same: one server, many recipients, several interchangeable servers, a goal of getting served.
   - The vehicle. Swap the bus for a checkout lane and the case keeps its parts, its relations, and its goal.
   - The scale, whether 40 riders a day or 4,000. The relation "one acknowledgment per person" holds either way.
   - The wave. A nod, a name, or a greeting is the same kind of part: a small personal act toward each recipient that the service itself does not require. The wave counts as a particular, and its kind stays.

   Must stay:

   - "Every single passenger." Drop it to "some passengers" and riders can no longer count on being acknowledged, so the reason to wait disappears.
   - "The one." Drop it to "all the drivers" and riders have nothing to choose between, so the case loses its comparison.
   - "Even when another comes first." Drop it and the riders pay nothing for the preference. The case then shows a liked driver, and it stops showing a preference that costs something.

2. What class does the route 12 driver's case belong to once those particulars are dropped, and which particulars were dropped to reach it?

   The class: one server, among servers who otherwise do the same job, gives every recipient a small discretionary act of personal regard, and recipients pay a real cost to be served by that one. A short name for it is "the unrequired acknowledgment that people pay to receive." Dropped to reach it: the route number, the city, the vehicle, the rider count, and the specific gesture of a wave.

   Other members: the barista who says each customer's name, the one checkout clerk who looks up and greets every person, the doctor's receptionist people time their calls around.

3. What is known to hold for every member of that class: solutions, failure modes, laws, limits?

   - The act carries weight because nothing requires it. A greeting that a rulebook mandates reads as procedure, and the recipient no longer reads it as regard for them.
   - Choosing the costlier option shows what the choice is worth to the chooser. Waiting through an earlier bus puts a floor under the value of the wave: at least as large as the wait.
   - The act is per person, and the giver's attention is what pays for it. So it fits a stream of recipients that arrives one at a time, and it shrinks or disappears when recipients arrive faster than attention can be spent.
   - The preference is fragile in three known ways. It hangs on one person, so a sick day, a shift swap, or a transfer removes it. It hangs on sincerity, so once riders suspect a motive such as tips or a review, its value falls. And it costs the giver something each time, so it can wear out the giver.
   - Preference for one server concentrates load on that server. Riders who skip the first bus leave it emptier and crowd the preferred one, and a crowd cuts the attention available to each person.
   - The act adds time per recipient, so a stop-by-stop greeting slows boarding and can make the bus run late.

4. Which of those items apply to the route 12 driver, and for each one set aside, which dropped particular voids it?

   Applies unchanged:

   - Value from being unrequired. Nothing about a transit route makes the wave a duty, so it counts.
   - The floor on the wave's value. The subject states that riders let an earlier bus pass, so the cost is observed.
   - The three fragilities. A route has shifts, so a different driver on the next run removes the preference. The suspicion effect applies to any gesture. The act costs the driver each time.
   - Load concentration. The riders who skip the first bus put more people on the driver's bus, and the wave gets shorter as boarding gets longer.
   - Boarding time. A wave at every stop adds seconds at every stop, so it can delay the run.

   Set aside:

   - "The server can turn the preference into a price." A barista can charge more or sell more. A transit fare is set by the agency, so the driver captures nothing. The dropped domain voids it. The benefit shows up as a full bus and riders who wait, and the driver never sees it as money.
   - "Recipients can see whose line they are joining." In a shop the clerk is visible before the customer commits. At a stop the driver is only visible when the bus arrives. Riders must therefore know the driver's shifts or run times. The dropped vehicle, and the shape of a fixed timetable, void this.

5. Does a wider class than the one named for the route 12 driver still predict anything about this driver's case?

   Yes. The wider class is "people pay real costs to be seen as an individual by someone whose regard is not for sale." Running the steps on it:

   - Step 1: the transit setting, the server role, and the gesture are dropped. What stays is an individual who receives an acknowledgment and a cost paid to receive it.
   - Step 2: the class is being recognised, treated as a good people pay for.
   - Step 3: what holds for the class is that recognition is worth more when it comes from someone others also want recognition from, that it is mutual (recipients who wave back or say thanks become part of it), and that a stop where many people have chosen to wait together turns into a small community around the giver.
   - Step 4: all of these apply. They predict two things the narrower class did not: riders at the stop probably wave and speak to each other and to the driver, and the preference survives the driver's occasional bad day but not the discovery of a motive. Neither prediction is checked against route 12 here.

   Running the steps on a still wider class, "people pay costs for things they value," adds nothing about this driver that the two classes above did not. The run stops here.
