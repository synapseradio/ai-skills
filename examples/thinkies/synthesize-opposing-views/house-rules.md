# synthesize-opposing-views: box rules versus house rules

- skill: synthesize-opposing-views, from commit 0c9258f
- subject: "Board games should be played by the rules in the box" versus "house rules are what make a game ours."
- kind: single run

## Prompt

/synthesize-opposing-views "Board games should be played by the rules in the box" versus "house rules are what make a game ours."

## Run

### 1. Identify the opposing positions

- Thesis: board games should be played by the rules in the box.
- Antithesis: house rules are what make a game ours.

Both are claims about where a game's authority lives. The thesis places it in the published text. The antithesis places it in the table that plays.

### 2. State the thesis clearly

**Core arguments**

- The rules are the game. A designer chose each rule to work with the others, and the box text is the only version every player can name. Change one rule and you play a different, untested game.
- A shared text gives strangers a common start. Two people who have never met can sit down and play the same game, and a tournament, a club night, or a review can compare like with like.
- Disputes have a court of appeal. When two players disagree, the page settles it, and nobody has to win an argument about who is more reasonable.
- Learning happens under constraint. A player who follows the printed rules meets the designed difficulty, including the parts that feel bad at first. Skipping those parts hides why the game was built as it was.

**What it explains well**

- Why a game with a strong design survives for decades: its balance was tuned as a whole.
- Why a house rule so often breaks something unexpectedly. A common example is putting money in the middle of the table in a property-trading game, so that a free-parking square pays it out to a lucky player. Players commonly report that this feeds cash into a game whose ending depends on cash running out, and that it can lengthen the game.
- Why a group that plays "by the book" often has fewer arguments at the table.

**Its appeal**

It is humble toward the designer and fair toward the newcomer. It offers a clear answer to "are we playing this right?"

### 3. Develop the antithesis

**Core arguments**

- A game is finished only when played. A box holds a text; a table holds people with particular ages, patience, moods, and memories. The rules that fit a room of strangers may not fit two siblings and a grandparent.
- Adjusting a game is a creative act, and a group that does so has invested in it. A rule invented at the table carries a story ("we added that the year it rained all weekend"), and the story is what makes the game belong to that group.
- Designers write for an average table that does not exist. Playtest groups are small, and a printed rule set is a compromise between many audiences. A group that knows its own members can do better for itself than the compromise does.
- Rule text has gaps and errors. Publishers issue errata and clarifications, and many players fill unclear rules with local rulings anyway. Some house-ruling is unavoidable, so the question is whether it is done consciously.

**What it explains well**

- Why the same game feels different at different tables, and why people say "our version."
- Why house rules spread and persist, including ones that a designer would call mistakes: they solve a local problem, such as a game running too long for a young player or a runaway leader discouraging everyone else.
- Why the hobby has a large culture of variants, expansions, and fan modifications that publishers now sometimes adopt.

**What it reveals that the thesis missed**

The thesis treats the text as the game. The antithesis shows that the text is an instruction for producing an experience, and that a table can judge whether the experience arrived. A rule exists to serve a good evening; when it does not, a purely loyal player is keeping the instrument and losing the music.

### 4. Examine the conflict

**Where they contradict**

Only in one place: who may change a rule, and on what grounds. The thesis says the text has authority over the table. The antithesis says the table has authority over the text. If both claims are read as absolute, they cannot both hold.

**What each gets right**

- The thesis is right that rules interlock, so a change has costs the changer cannot always see, and that a shared text lets strangers play together.
- The antithesis is right that a game exists to be enjoyed by particular people, and that the text has gaps and imperfect fits.

**What each misses**

- The thesis misses that many players fail to reach the designed experience when they follow the text exactly. A rule set followed to the letter by a mismatched group produces a bad evening, and no designer wanted that outcome.
- The antithesis misses that a house rule made without understanding the rule it replaces often damages the thing the group wanted. Ownership of a game does not guarantee the changed game is better.

**Different questions, different assumptions**

- The thesis is answering: what makes a game comparable, teachable, and fair between people who do not know each other?
- The antithesis is answering: what makes a game valuable to the people who play it repeatedly?
- The thesis assumes that the designer's intent is the target. The antithesis assumes that the table's enjoyment is the target. Neither assumption is argued for by either side; each holds it as obvious.
- The thesis pictures a table of strangers or mixed skill. The antithesis pictures a stable group that has played together for years. Each picture is accurate for some tables.

### 5. Identify the deeper truth

**Is it a false dichotomy?** Largely yes. The two claims are not opposite answers to one question. They are answers to two questions about two different times in a game's life at a table.

**Are both partially right in different contexts?** Yes, and the contexts have a shape. The thesis is strongest at the start (a new game, a new player, a new group, a competition), where a shared text substitutes for shared history. The antithesis is strongest later, once a group has a history of its own and knows what the rules do.

**What the conflict reveals**

- A rule has two jobs. It is a mechanism that shapes play, and it is a shared agreement that lets people coordinate. The thesis defends the coordination job and the antithesis defends the mechanism job. A change to the mechanism is safe only when the agreement survives it.
- The important variable is understanding, not fidelity. The players who house-rule well are the ones who first learned the game as printed, so they know what each rule was doing. The players who house-rule badly are the ones who altered rules they had never felt the effect of.
- "Ours" needs a "before". A group cannot make something its own without a thing to make its own. The box rules are the material from which the group's version is made, and this is the reason the two positions depend on each other.

### 6. Construct the synthesis

**Position: the box rules are the baseline that a table earns the right to depart from, by knowing what it is departing from, agreeing on the departure, and being open about it.**

Put as working principles:

1. **Play it as printed first.** Play the game by the box until the group can say what each rule is for. This is when the thesis governs, and it is the price of admission to the second stage.
2. **Change a rule to solve a named problem.** A house rule answers a specific complaint ("the leader always wins by turn three", "it takes too long for the youngest player"). A rule with no named problem is a habit, and habits are the source of the damage the thesis warns about.
3. **Keep the change to the agreement, not just the mechanism.** Everyone at the table knows and accepts the variant before play begins. This carries the coordination job of the thesis into the house rule. A rule sprung mid-game, or held by one person, is a way to win rather than a way to play.
4. **Label the variant.** When the group plays with outsiders, or teaches a new person, say "we play with a change" and state it, and offer the printed version. The text remains the common language; the house rule is a dialect, and a dialect is honest when it is named.
5. **Keep the baseline reachable.** A group that can still play the printed game, and knows how to, has both the shared language and the local one. A group that has forgotten the box has lost the ability to play with anyone else.

This explains what neither position explains alone. It says why house rules delight some groups and ruin the games of others (understanding and agreement, not the existence of the change). It says why tournaments and teaching tables rightly insist on the box (the coordination job dominates when there is no shared history). And it says why the same person can be a purist at a convention and a tinkerer at home without any inconsistency: the two settings ask the rule to do different jobs.

### 7. Test the synthesis

**Does it account for what each side got right?**

- Thesis: yes. Rules interlock (principle 2 requires a named problem, which forces the group to look at what the rule did). Strangers need a shared text (principles 4 and 5 preserve one). Disputes have an appeal court (principle 3 fixes the rules before play, so the court is still the page or the agreement, never the loudest voice).
- Antithesis: yes. The group's ownership is preserved in principle 2 and its story is preserved in the naming of the variant. Local needs justify departure. Gaps in the text are covered, since a gap is a named problem.

**Does it resolve the original conflict?** It resolves it by sequence and by purpose, not by splitting the difference. It does not claim the truth lies halfway between the two. It claims each position governs a different moment and a different job, and it gives a test for which moment the table is in.

**New tensions and questions**

- **How long is "first"?** The principle asks for play as printed before departure, but gives no number. A game with a long learning curve may need many plays, and a group with a child in the room may not have the time. The synthesis names the condition (knowing what each rule is for) but a group cannot always tell whether it meets it.
- **Who counts as the table?** Agreement is easy with equals and harder when one player is a child, a guest, or someone who dislikes speaking up. A rule everyone "agreed to" under social pressure satisfies the form and not the substance of principle 3.
- **Is a designer's intent binding on a game whose designer is gone, or whose publisher has changed it?** The synthesis treats the box as a baseline. It does not say whether some games, such as abstract strategy games with a single tight balance, deserve stronger deference than a party game built to be bent.
- **Does the earned-departure account flatter experienced players?** It is possible that novices, by ignorance, produce house rules that improve the game, and the synthesis has no way to credit that case beyond noting that the named-problem test still applies to them.
- **Open question the synthesis leaves standing:** is a game that a group has altered enough still the same game, or has the group made a new one and the loyalty on both sides was aimed at the wrong object all along?
