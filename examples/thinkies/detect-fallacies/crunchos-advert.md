# detect-fallacies: a cereal advert claiming astronaut endorsement

- skill: detect-fallacies, from commit 0c9258f
- subject: A cereal advert: "Nine out of ten astronauts' kids eat Crunchos. Astronauts are the smartest people alive. Every other cereal is just sugar in disguise. Don't let your child be the one who doesn't reach the stars."
- kind: single run

## Prompt

/detect-fallacies A cereal advert: "Nine out of ten astronauts' kids eat Crunchos. Astronauts are the smartest people alive. Every other cereal is just sugar in disguise. Don't let your child be the one who doesn't reach the stars."

## Run

### 1. The argument

The target is the four-sentence advert quoted above. Its conclusion is never stated outright. It is the instruction a buyer is meant to take away: buy Crunchos for your child.

### 2. Structure

- **Claim (implied):** Buy Crunchos for your child.
- **Premise 1:** Nine out of ten astronauts' kids eat Crunchos.
- **Premise 2:** Astronauts are the smartest people alive.
- **Premise 3:** Every other cereal is just sugar in disguise.
- **Premise 4 (a threat, not a premise):** If you don't, your child may be the one who doesn't reach the stars.

The connective reasoning runs like this. Smart people's households choose Crunchos (P1 with P2). The alternatives are junk (P3). Therefore choosing Crunchos is the smart, protective choice, and refusing it risks your child's future (P4). Two links carry the weight. The first runs from "astronauts' kids eat it" to "it is good for a child". The second runs from "good for a child" to "your child's prospects depend on it". Neither link is argued for.

### 3. Fallacy patterns found

From the skill's list:

- **Appeal to authority.** P1 and P2 exist to borrow the prestige of astronauts. Nobody claims the astronauts know anything about cereal, nutrition, or child development. Being smart at orbital mechanics is offered as expert opinion on breakfast. The authority is also secondhand: it is the astronauts' children who eat the cereal, and the children have no authority at all.
- **Hasty generalization.** "Nine out of ten astronauts' kids" is a fraction of a very small group. Astronauts are a very small group, and their children are a smaller one. The advert gives no count, so "nine out of ten" could be nine children out of ten asked, and the conclusion is meant to reach every family.
- **Correlation as causation.** Astronauts' kids eat Crunchos, and astronauts' kids are presumed to do well. The advert invites the reverse reading: Crunchos is part of what produces the success. Family income, schooling, parental attention, and simple chance are all left out.
- **Appeal to emotion.** "Don't let your child be the one who doesn't reach the stars" does its work through fear of a parent's failure and guilt. It carries no evidence about cereal.
- **False dichotomy.** P3 sorts every cereal on the market into two bins: Crunchos, and sugar in disguise. It says nothing about oat-based, low-sugar, or wholegrain products, and nothing about how much sugar Crunchos contains.
- **Survivorship bias.** The advert cites the children who reached the stars, or are presumed to, and never asks about astronauts' kids who ate Crunchos and did not, or who ate something else and did. The population is chosen after the outcome.

Not on the skill's list, but present:

- **Bandwagon.** "Nine out of ten" tells the buyer that the sensible people have already chosen.
- **Poisoning the well.** "Just sugar in disguise" disposes of every competitor in one phrase, without naming any of them or the product's own sugar content.

### 4. The specific flaw

The break is between P1 and the conclusion. The advert never says why an astronaut's child's breakfast should be evidence of quality for anyone else. That gap is filled with status.

Three things are assumed incorrectly:

1. That admired people's families choose products for reasons that track product quality. Families may choose Crunchos because a sponsor supplied it, or because the children like it.
2. That "smartest people alive" is true, and that it transfers to a household purchase. P2 is asserted as a fact and never supported. It is also unclear who counts as "smartest": astronauts are selected on health, training, and temperament as well as intellect.
3. That the choice of cereal has a measurable effect on a child's future. The closing line makes this the entire emotional payload, and the advert offers nothing toward it.

Three pieces of relevant information are missing or hidden:

- The sample size and how the ten were chosen.
- Whether Crunchos is itself high in sugar, which would turn P3 back on the product.
- Whether the astronauts' families were paid or supplied.

### 5. Steelman

Before flagging everything, what would make this reasoning valid?

- **Reading it as a taste survey.** If the claim is only "nine of ten in this small group eat it", it is true, could be checked, and asserts nothing false. The fault lies in what the sentence is used to imply. This pushes the flag toward implicature, not toward the literal claim.
- **Reading astronauts as informed consumers.** Astronauts spend careers in a culture of data, risk assessment, and careful diet. A parent from that culture may weigh nutrition with unusual care. That would make P1 weak but real evidence. It still does not survive the step from "informed parents" to "children's behaviour", since children choose their own cereal.
- **Reading "sugar in disguise" as advertising hyperbole.** Most readers do not take it literally, and the law treats such puffery leniently. It becomes a fallacy only where it is doing the work of a premise, and here it is.
- **Reading the last line as a joke.** The register of the advert is playful. That protects the line from a charge of literal fear-mongering. It does not protect the reasoning that a buyer might act on.

After steelmanning, the stronger version of the advert is: "Children in a small, notable group eat this cereal, and we think you might like it." That version commits no fallacy, and it also fails to persuade, which suggests the fallacies are the persuasion.

The flags that hold after this pass are appeal to authority (by proxy), hasty generalization, correlation as causation, false dichotomy, and appeal to emotion. Survivorship bias is the weakest flag. It depends on the advert implying that the astronauts' kids succeeded, and the text implies this only through the closing line.

### 6. Explanation and corrections

**Why the reasoning fails.** The advert has one real fact (a small fraction of a small group eats the product) and builds three unsupported claims on it: the group is smart, smart means well-informed about cereal, and cereal decides whether a child succeeds. Each step moves further from evidence and closer to feeling. A counter-example makes the transfer visible: nine out of ten test pilots' kids may also drink a particular brand of orange juice, and nobody would conclude the juice makes a pilot.

**Counter-example for P3.** A plain oat cereal with no added sugar exists on most shelves, and its existence is enough to falsify "every other cereal".

**How it could be strengthened.** An honest version would replace each fallacy with something a buyer could check:

- State the sample: "We asked 10 families of astronauts. Nine said their children eat Crunchos."
- Drop the claim that astronauts are the smartest people alive. Keep the fact that they are trained and carefully selected, if that matters.
- Name the sugar content per serving beside the competitors' figures. A real comparison replaces "sugar in disguise".
- Cut the closing threat, or replace it with a claim about the product itself, such as "a bowl with fibre and less sugar than the leading brand".

**What the buyer can do.** Ask three questions of any advert with this shape: who is being used as the authority, what do they actually know about the product, and what would the claim look like with the prestige removed.
