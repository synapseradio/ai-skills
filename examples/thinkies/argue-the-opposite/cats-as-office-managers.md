# argue-the-opposite: cats as office managers

- skill: argue-the-opposite, from commit 0c9258f
- subject: Cats are better office managers than people.
- kind: single run

## Prompt

/argue-the-opposite Cats are better office managers than people.

## Run

### 1. State the position

Cats are better office managers than people. The case for it runs as follows. A cat manages by presence. It does not call meetings, it does not micro-manage, and it does not take credit. It steers foot traffic, since people gather where it sits. It gives a team a shared, low-stakes topic that lowers the temperature between colleagues. It has no ambition to climb, no politics to play, and no salary to negotiate. A human manager brings all of that overhead, so the office is better off with the cat.

### 2. Adopt the skeptic

I am a capable skeptic who has run teams and has also lived with cats. I grant that the cat is pleasant. I dispute that pleasant is the same as managing. I will treat "office manager" as a job with duties, and test the cat against the duties.

### 3. Build the counter-argument

**Factual.** An office manager's work is mostly small, unglamorous acts of coordination. Someone orders the supplies, renews the lease, reconciles the invoices, calls the plumber, books the fire inspection, and tells the new hire where the keys are. A cat performs none of these. It has no hands for the door code, no language for the vendor, no access to the account. The cat's contribution is real, and it is a contribution to mood. Mood is one input to how an office runs, and it is not the office.

**Logical.** The position rests on a hidden swap. It starts from "cats are pleasant to have around" and ends at "cats manage better." The premises support the first claim. Each premise on the list (no meetings, no politics, no credit-taking) names a fault a human manager can have, and treats the cat's inability to do the work as the absence of those faults. An absent fault is not a performed duty. By the same reasoning, an empty chair is a better manager than any person, since it also holds no meetings and takes no credit. If the argument proves the empty chair wins, the argument proves too much.

**Practical.** Management is an accountability role. When the heating fails at nine on a Monday, someone owns the phone call. When two colleagues are in conflict, someone hears both sides and decides. When a person is struggling, someone notices from the pattern of their week and speaks to them. A cat can be comforting in each of those moments. It cannot own any of them, and it cannot be asked, afterward, what it decided and why. An office run on the cat's presence still needs a person doing every duty, and that person is now the manager in everything except title, and carries the work with no authority to match. The cat has not removed the manager. It has hidden one.

There is a second practical cost. Cats are also not neutral. Some colleagues are allergic. Some are afraid of cats. Some cultural and religious observances shape contact with animals. A person who is hired to manage can be trained, replaced, or held to a standard. A cat's presence excludes some people from the room and gives them no recourse, because there is nobody to appeal to. Warmth for most of the office is bought with a cost that falls on a few.

**Historical.** Offices and workplaces have kept animals for a long time, usually for a single duty. Mousing cats on ships, in warehouses, and in shops did a specific job, and did it well. Where a cat was kept, it was kept to catch mice, and nobody mistook it for the person who kept the books. The record of cats at work is a record of cats doing cat work. It gives no support to the claim that a cat can do the work of a person, and it suggests the opposite: the arrangement lasted where the duties stayed separate.

### 4. Present alternatives

The position picks up a real problem and offers one answer to it. The problem is that many managers add friction: too many meetings, too much surveillance, too little trust. Several alternatives rest on different assumptions and keep the human.

- **Lighter management, still human.** Assume the goal is fewer meetings and less oversight, and that the manager's job is to remove obstacles. A manager measured by how little friction the team feels replaces the cat's quiet with a person's judgment.
- **Rotating or shared coordination.** Assume the duties are small and can be split. The team divides supplies, vendors, and onboarding among themselves. No single manager exists to add overhead, and every duty still has an owner.
- **Automate the routine, keep a person for the exceptions.** Assume most of the work is repeatable. Ordering, renewals, and reconciliation move to tools, and a person handles the phone call, the conflict, and the struggling colleague, which are the parts that need someone who can be held to account.
- **Keep the cat, and call it what it is.** Assume the cat's value is real and separate. The cat joins the office as a morale asset next to a manager, with a plan for the colleagues who cannot share the room with it. The claim shrinks from "better manager" to "good addition," and the claim that remains is one the evidence can carry.

### 5. Concede what's right

The opposition is right about the target, and the position was right to aim there. Some human managers do add more friction than they remove, and an office can drift into meetings that serve the calendar. The cat is also right about something: presence without agenda changes how a room feels, and a team that shares a small source of warmth talks to each other more easily. If the position is read as a claim about what the cat gives the room, the opposition has no quarrel with it.

The opposition is not right that the cat is irrelevant. It would be wrong to say a manager's warmth and a cat's warmth are the same thing. They differ, and the cat's version costs the office nothing in politics. I did not find an argument that the cat's mood effect is illusory, and I would not make one.

### 6. Decide

Refine. The position as stated, "cats are better office managers than people," does not survive. It proves too much (the empty-chair test), and it leaves every duty of the role unowned. Abandoning it would throw out the true part, which is that a cat improves the mood of a room and a bad manager makes it worse. Maintaining it would ask a reader to accept that the absence of a fault is the presence of a skill.

The refined position: **a cat is a good addition to an office and a poor replacement for its manager. It beats a bad manager on mood and loses to any competent one on everything else.** The claim that stays is narrower and can be checked: measure what the office needs done, and give the cat the duty it performs, which is presence.

What the opposition revealed is that the original claim was two claims joined. One is about mood, where the cat has a case. The other is about duties, where the cat has none. Keeping them apart is the outcome of the exercise.
