# integrate-other-perspectives: beach, museum, or a bench with tea

- skill: integrate-other-perspectives, from commit 0c9258f
- subject: Saturday outing. The eight-year-old wants the beach ("there are crabs"); the parent wants the museum ("it's free and it might rain"); the grandparent wants "somewhere with a bench and a proper cup of tea".
- kind: single run

## Prompt

/integrate-other-perspectives Saturday outing. The eight-year-old wants the beach ("there are crabs"); the parent wants the museum ("it's free and it might rain"); the grandparent wants "somewhere with a bench and a proper cup of tea".

## Run

### 1. Identify the perspectives

Three people, three stated wishes, one Saturday:

- The eight-year-old: the beach.
- The parent: the museum.
- The grandparent: somewhere with a bench and a proper cup of tea.

### 2. Lay out what is being combined

Each piece in its own terms, before any simplifying.

**The eight-year-old: "the beach, there are crabs."**

- Origin: the child's own wish, with a reason attached. The reason is crabs, not sand or sea.
- Individual value: the wish names a living thing to find. It asks for looking, crouching, lifting things, and being surprised. It is an activity with a payoff, and it needs no entertainment supplied by anyone else.
- What the words leave open: whether "the beach" means this one place with a rock pool, or any shore. Whether crabs means catching, watching, or counting.

**The parent: "the museum, it's free and it might rain."**

- Origin: the person who carries the logistics and the risk. Both reasons are about constraints, not about wanting the museum itself.
- Individual value: two real protections. Cost: free means the outing cannot become an expense. Weather: a roof means the day survives rain.
- What the words leave open: the museum is offered as the answer to two problems, money and rain. Nothing here says the parent wants museum contents. The wish is really "an outing that is cheap and does not fail in bad weather."

**The grandparent: "somewhere with a bench and a proper cup of tea."**

- Origin: the person whose body sets limits on the day. Sitting and a hot drink are needs for comfort and pace.
- Individual value: this is the only wish that names no place. It names two conditions: a place to sit, and tea done properly. Any location meeting them qualifies.
- What the words leave open: how far the bench may be from the action, and how far "proper" stretches (a cafe, a flask, a kiosk).

Stated so, the three wishes differ in kind. One names a place and a reason. One names a place to serve two constraints. One names no place at all, only conditions.

### 3. Find points of contact

- **Beach and grandparent.** A beach can hold a bench, and watching a child crouch at a rock pool is a good use of one. Crabs are found by staying still and looking, which suits a person who is sitting. The grandparent can be the fixed point the child reports back to.
- **Museum and grandparent.** Museums usually have benches, and often a cafe. The tea condition may be easiest to meet indoors.
- **Museum and eight-year-old.** Some museums have natural history galleries. Crabs, shells, or a rock pool exhibit could stand in for the crabs. Whether this museum has any is unknown.
- **Beach and parent.** The parent's cost concern is met by a beach as well: a beach is free too. What a beach fails is the rain constraint.
- **What one explains about another.** The parent's two reasons explain why the museum was offered: it is a solution to cost and weather. Once that is seen, the museum is one candidate answer to "free and rain-proof," and the beach is another candidate for "free." That reframes the parent's position from "the museum" to "free, and safe from rain."
- **The child's reason is portable.** "There are crabs" says what the child wants: to find crabs. The beach is where the child knows crabs to be. The wish is for crabs, attached to a place by the child's knowledge so far.

### 4. Address tensions

**Beach versus museum.** This is the surface conflict. Are they real or apparent?

- Real in one respect: they are different places, and an afternoon cannot be spent fully at both without a long day.
- Apparent in another: the parent's reasons for the museum are cost and rain, and the beach meets the cost. The only live disagreement is weather. That makes the conflict conditional. If the day is dry, the parent's objection to the beach mostly falls away. If it rains, the beach fails the child and the grandparent's bench too.
- Both can hold in different contexts. The choice depends on a fact nobody at the table has yet: what the weather does on Saturday.

**Rain versus crabs.** The child's payoff depends on being outside, at low tide or at least at the water's edge, and the parent's precaution depends on being inside. This tension is real for a rainy Saturday. The tension also reveals something: the parent's plan is a fallback, and the child's wish is the first choice. Nobody has said which is which.

**Bench versus crab-hunting.** The grandparent wants to sit, and the child wants to crouch at rock pools, which are usually at the water's edge and may be some way from the bench. This is a real tension of distance, not of wish. The question it raises is where the bench sits relative to the crabs, and whether the grandparent can see the child from it.

**Tea versus everything.** Tea is the cheapest wish to meet, since a flask can travel to any of these places. That makes the "proper cup of tea" a lever: it can be satisfied without settling where the day goes. Whether a flask counts as proper is the grandparent's call.

**What the tensions reveal.** Only one tension is about the places themselves: weather. The rest are about the arrangement inside a place: distance from bench to crabs, and where tea comes from. The argument at the table is framed as beach against museum, but most of it is about details a chosen place can settle.

### 5. Build the synthesis

**The larger picture.** Three constraints and one desire.

- One desire: the child wants to find crabs.
- Three constraints on the day: it costs nothing, it survives rain, and it includes a seat and hot tea.

Read this way, nobody is asking for opposing things. The child names what the day is for, the parent names how the day must not fail, and the grandparent names what the day must give the person who cannot stand for hours. A day is wanted that has crabs in it, does not cost, does not collapse in rain, and has somewhere to sit with tea.

**What emerges that was in no single piece.** A plan structured as one main choice plus a fallback, both built from the same three conditions:

- **Dry day.** The beach, at a spot with a rock pool and a bench in view of it, with tea brought in a flask or bought nearby. Crabs are the point of the day, the grandparent is seated, cost is nil or low.
- **Wet day.** The museum, with a bench and its cafe, and with the child given a crab errand inside: a gallery hunt for shells, crabs, or sea creatures, so the wish carries over even indoors.
- **Deciding.** Look at the forecast on Saturday morning and take whichever branch it picks. The decision moves from three people's preferences to one observable fact.

**The organizing principle.** Treat each person's statement as a requirement on the day, not as a vote for a place. The child's requirement is "crabs are found." The parent's is "free, and rain does not end the outing." The grandparent's is "a seat and proper tea." A plan is good when it meets all three requirements, and here it takes one weather-dependent choice between two places to do so.

### 6. Test coherence

- **Holds together logically?** Yes for the branch structure: each branch meets all three requirements on its own terms. The dry branch meets crab, cost, and seat; the wet branch meets cost, rain, and seat, and only partly meets crabs.
- **Preserves what matters from each piece?**
    - Child: kept in full on a dry day. Kept only as a substitute (a crab hunt indoors) on a wet day. That is a real loss, and the plan says so.
    - Parent: kept in full. Cost and rain are both covered, since the museum is the wet-day branch.
    - Grandparent: kept in full in both branches, provided a bench exists in view of the crabs. That proviso is unverified.
- **Creates new understanding?** Yes. It replaces "beach versus museum" with "which of two places meets the same three requirements today," and it shows the weather, not any person, as the decider.
- **Explains simply?** One sentence: go to the beach if it is dry and the museum if it rains, with crabs, a bench, and tea in both. It does. The one thing it cannot do is make a wet-day museum trip as good for the child as crabs, and the synthesis does not hide that.

### 7. Check what's missing

**Gaps.**

- Nobody has said what the forecast is. The whole structure hangs on it.
- Whether a beach with a rock pool exists within reach, and whether it has a bench in sight of the water. This is a fact to look up, not a preference to discuss.
- Whether the museum has anything the child would count as crabs. If it has nothing, the wet-day branch serves the parent and grandparent and fails the child.
- What the grandparent means by "proper." A flask may or may not satisfy it.

**What does not fit.**

- Travel time and tide. The plan treats both places as reachable and the crabs as findable. A beach at high tide may show no rock pool. Crabs may not appear on demand. Either could sink the dry branch.
- The child's wish was "the beach" and a reason. The plan honors the reason. It does not know whether the child would accept a museum crab hunt as the same thing or as a consolation.

**New questions.**

- To the child: is it finding crabs, holding them, or watching them? The answer changes what a museum can offer.
- To the parent: is the museum a preference or purely a hedge against cost and rain? If a free dry-weather option exists, does the parent still want it?
- To the grandparent: how far from the bench can the crabs be, and is tea from a flask acceptable?
- To all three: if it rains and the museum has nothing crab-shaped, is a second, drier Saturday better than a worse outing today?
