# branch-possibilities: a lighthouse and a million rubber ducks

- skill: branch-possibilities, from commit 0c9258f
- subject: A small coastal town inherits a decommissioned lighthouse and one million rubber ducks. What could it do?
- kind: single run

## Prompt

/branch-possibilities A small coastal town inherits a decommissioned lighthouse and one million rubber ducks. What could it do?

## Run

### 1. The starting point

The town holds two assets it did not choose: a tower on the shore with no navigational job left, and a stock of one million identical ducks. Neither comes with instructions.

The decision that can be altered is what the town takes the inheritance to be. Every direction below answers that question differently. Each one also sets a different answer to who benefits (visitors, the town budget, the sea, the residents) and to how long the effort lasts (a season, a decade, once).

Constraints that stay fixed across all branches:

- The town is small, so staff, budget, and volunteer hours are scarce.
- The ducks are a finite stock. A duck used in one branch is not available to another.
- The lighthouse is a fixed site on the coast. Its upkeep cost exists whatever the town decides, and the town has not yet learned how large that cost is.

### 2. Divergent directions

Six directions, chosen because each treats the inheritance as a different kind of thing.

1. **Destination.** The inheritance is an attraction. The lighthouse becomes the venue and the ducks become the exhibit.
2. **Liquidation.** The inheritance is money. The town sells or leases both assets and takes the proceeds.
3. **Instrument.** The inheritance is equipment. The ducks are floating, numbered, cheap drifters, and the lighthouse is a fixed observation post.
4. **Ritual.** The inheritance is a shared occasion. The ducks power one recurring public event, and the lighthouse is its backdrop.
5. **Conversion.** The inheritance is raw material. The town turns the tower into something people live or work in, and turns the ducks into something other than ducks.
6. **Transfer.** The inheritance is a burden better carried by someone else. The town hands it to a body built to hold it and keeps a stake in the outcome.

### 3. Each branch made concrete

**1. Destination**

- In practice: the lighthouse gets a paid entry, a lamp room viewing deck, and a duck hall. Visitors climb past a spiral of ducks, and a small shop sells ducks from the stock. A town run ticket booth and a café sit at the base.
- What differs from today: the town needs opening hours, insurance, staff, and a season. The tower needs to be made safe for public stairs.
- Trade-offs: recurring revenue is possible, but only if visitors come, and a small town has no control over that. The upkeep becomes an operating business, and the town carries the risk of a bad year. A million ducks are far more than a hall can display, so most of the stock sits in storage or leaves through the shop.

**2. Liquidation**

- In practice: the town lists the ducks in lots to retailers, event organizers, and collectors, and leases the lighthouse to a private operator (lodging, gallery, or lookout).
- What differs from today: the town stops owning either thing within a year or two. Its work is procurement and contracts, not operation.
- Trade-offs: fastest route to cash and the smallest ongoing burden. The price of a million identical ducks is unknown, and a single large sale may fetch far less per duck than a slow trickle. The town gives up control over what happens to the tower, and residents may dislike a private operator on their shoreline.

**3. Instrument**

- In practice: each duck gets a serial number and a return address. Ducks are released in batches at planned times and places, and people who find them report where. The lighthouse hosts the release schedule, the reports desk, and a wall map. A school or a university partner supplies the questions.
- What differs from today: the town becomes a data source and a teaching site instead of a seller or a host.
- Trade-offs: it produces knowledge about local currents and drift, and it gives the town a reason for outsiders to write to it. The cost is that released ducks are plastic left in the sea. That makes release size the central decision: a few hundred ducks tracked by volunteers is defensible; a million is not. A small release therefore leaves most of the stock idle, so this branch alone does not dispose of the inheritance.

**4. Ritual**

- In practice: one annual day. Ducks go into a channel, harbor, or pool, and the first across the line wins. Sponsors buy "adoptions" of individual ducks, and each adoption is the ticket. The same ducks are collected and reused every year.
- What differs from today: the effort concentrates in one day a year. The lighthouse is lit or opened for that day only.
- Trade-offs: low fixed cost and high community feeling, and the reused stock makes a million ducks last indefinitely. Revenue is seasonal and small compared with branch 1, and the day depends on weather and volunteers. The lighthouse still needs upkeep for the other 364 days.

**5. Conversion**

- In practice: the tower becomes housing for a resident keeper, artist studios, or a rentable stay. The ducks go to a recycler, or to a maker who turns them into new products, if the material allows it. If it does not, they go into a licensed disposal stream.
- What differs from today: neither asset stays what it was. The town ends up with a working building and no duck stock.
- Trade-offs: it puts both assets to durable practical use, and the ducks stop being a storage problem. It also destroys the thing that makes the inheritance unusual, and it gives up the tourism value of the ducks as ducks. The ducks' material, and whether it can be recycled, is unverified.

**6. Transfer**

- In practice: the town gives the lighthouse to a lighthouse preservation society, a national heritage body, or a community trust, and gives the ducks to a charity, a hospital play program, or a museum. The town keeps a seat on the trust and a naming credit.
- What differs from today: almost everything. The town's job is negotiation and paperwork, then oversight.
- Trade-offs: the cheapest branch in money and time, and it hands the tower to people who know how to keep one standing. The town gives up revenue and control, and the receiving body may itself be stretched. Whether the will or gift terms allow a transfer is unknown.

### 4. The divergence map

Where the paths split:

- **Keep or release.** Branches 1, 3, and 4 keep ownership of the ducks. Branches 2, 5, and 6 give it up. This is the first split and the hardest to reverse. A sold or scrapped duck cannot come back.
- **Operate the tower or not.** Branches 1, 4, and 5 have the town in the building. Branches 2 and 6 move the building to someone else. Branch 3 needs the tower only lightly.
- **Purpose of the ducks.** Display (1), trade (2), measurement (3), play (4), material (5), gift (6). Each branch uses the ducks as something different, which is why they compete for the same stock.

Where branches can recombine:

- **1 with 4.** The duck hall is open all year, and the annual race is its event day. The same ducks serve both, and the race supplies the attendance the hall needs.
- **1 with 3.** The exhibit can explain the drift study, and a small tracked release becomes a feature of the hall.
- **4 with 3.** Race ducks carry numbers, and adoptions double as a tracking program if a limited batch is released each year.
- **2 with 1.** The town keeps a display set of ducks and sells the rest, which turns the surplus into funding for the tower.
- **6 with anything.** Transfer of the tower to a trust can sit alongside a town run duck event.

Where branches cannot recombine:

- **5 with 1, 3, or 4** on the ducks. Once the ducks are converted, no display, study, or race can use them.
- **2 with 4 or 3** on the ducks at scale. A sold stock is not available for reuse.
- **1 with 5** on the tower. A public attraction and a private residence compete for the same rooms.

Reading the map: the town's first real decision is whether to keep the ducks. Everything about the tower can be settled later, and most of the branches that keep the ducks combine well. The branches that release the ducks are the ones that close off the others.
