# what-if: a corner bakery meets a supermarket bakery

- skill: what-if, from commit 0c9258f
- subject: A corner bakery (two staff, famous for its cinnamon knots) learns that a supermarket with an in-store bakery opens across the street in four months.
- kind: staged conversation (the user side played by a second agent)

## Prompt

/what-if A corner bakery (two staff, famous for its cinnamon knots) learns that a supermarket with an in-store bakery opens across the street in four months.

## Run

**Q1. What am I looking at, and what bounds it?**

Two people run a bakery that sells bread, pastry and one famous product, the cinnamon knot. The parts feed each other: the knots draw the foot traffic, and the foot traffic sells the bread and coffee that sit beside them. So the knots are the part to protect, and the rest rides on them.

The situation moves through three phases. Now to opening day gives four months of preparation. Opening day to about six months after it is the stretch when customers try the newcomer. After that comes a settled pattern.

The constraints every future must respect:

- Four months to opening is fixed; the supermarket has announced it.
- Two staff means a hard ceiling on hours and on what one oven can bake before dawn.
- The supermarket's prices, hours and parking are not something the bakery controls.
- Whatever the bakery does with its recipe, its street, and its regulars can happen only inside those two pairs of hands.

**Q2. What do I know, and what would help to know?**

- **Known:** the supermarket is coming in four months; it sits across the street; it has an in-store bakery; the bakery has two staff and a signature product.
- **Knowable:** what an in-store bakery sells and how its shoppers behave, which I looked up. A trade-press report on a 2018 industry study says indulgent items make up 76% of in-store bakery sales, and about 37% of shoppers buy indulgent bakery items somewhere else, such as a specialty store or a stand-alone bakery ([Grocery Dive](https://www.grocerydive.com/news/bakery-departments-are-driving-store-trips-report-says/552282)). That is a trade-press summary of a United States survey from 2018, so it sits below the publisher rung and shows a pattern, not this street. It tells me the knot competes on the supermarket's strongest ground, and that a real share of shoppers leave that ground for a specialist. I searched for evidence on what an opening does to a nearby independent bakery's sales and found none; the one academic hit concerned food deserts and does not apply. What this particular chain puts on its counter I could not look up, so it stays open below.
- **Knowable if:** whether regulars stay once the newcomer is a hundred steps away; whether the supermarket puts a cinnamon roll of its own on the counter; whether the supermarket's foot traffic spills over to the bakery or drains from it. Each resolves only when customers and the chain make their moves. These are the axis candidates for Q3.
- **It would help to know:** facts only the owners hold, which set how much of the bakery the arrival can reach and how much room there is to act. I cannot look these up, so I catalogue them and ask.

The catalogue, as [references/asking.md](references/asking.md) prescribes, with each item's reason and the branch its answer selects:

1. Share of weekly takings that comes from cinnamon knots. It decides whether the arrival hits the core of the business or its edge. A high share routes toward protecting the knot at all costs; a low share routes toward protecting the rest of the counter.
2. How many knots one morning's bake can reach with the current oven and the two of them. It decides whether "sell more knots" is a strategy at all or a ceiling already hit.
3. Months of the bakery's running costs that savings and the current month's takings could cover. It decides whether a slow first half-year is survivable, which selects between a phased commitment and an all-in one.
4. When the lease next comes up for renewal. It decides whether the street is a fixed condition or a choice that can still change.

An item whose every answer leads to the same place would have left the catalogue. These four each select a branch, so all four stay.

**Skill:** Four small things would let me draw the right futures for the bakery, and every honest answer is a useful one :)

1. Out of a typical week's takings, roughly what share comes from the cinnamon knots?
2. On a good morning, about how many knots can the two of you bake before opening, and does anything (oven, dough rest, hands) stop you at that number?
3. If takings dipped, how many months of the bakery's running costs could savings and a normal month's takings carry?
4. What date does the lease next come up, and is there a renewal option after it?

Rough numbers are plenty. If any of these has no answer yet, say so and I will carry a named assumption in its place and mark what changes if it turns out wrong.

**User (played):** Cinnamon knots are about 40% of what we take in, so nearly half the till. On a good morning the two of us can do at most 180 before opening, and the oven is what stops us. We can't fit more in.

Savings cover about five months of running costs. Normal takings would stretch that a bit if they dipped rather than vanished, but I don't know yet by how much.

The lease comes up in about 14 months, so late November next year, and there's a renewal option after it.

Every item answered, so nothing needs a stated assumption. Known now: knots are about 40% of takings, the oven caps knots at 180 a morning, savings carry about five months, and the lease renews in 14 months with an option. The "knowable if" pile holds three entries, so the route goes to Q3.

**Q3. Which of the "knowable if" entries would change what the bakery does?**

All three candidates in one pass first: regulars stay or drift; the supermarket counter carries a rival cinnamon roll or does not; spillover traffic helps or drains. Seen together, the first and third share a cause: both ask whether people who cross the street for groceries come back across it for a knot. I fold spillover into the first entry.

Two entries remain, and each changes the move:

- **Loyalty.** If knot buyers stay for the knot, the danger is the bread and coffee that ride beside it. If they drift to the one-stop shop, the knot itself is at risk. Different threats, different defenses.
- **The rival roll.** If the supermarket has no cinnamon roll, the knot is uncontested and the fight is about everything else. If it has one, the knot's edge has to be the craft, since the price will be lower.

Two axes, so the full chain at two-axis depth. The lease and the oven are answered facts, so they stay off the axes. The bakery could buy a second oven or trim its counter, but those are strategies the owners control, so they go to Q9 and not to the axes.

**Q4. Do the axes vary independently?**

Hold the rival roll fixed at "present." Loyalty can still land either way: regulars who value the hand-made knot can stay even beside a cheaper roll, and casual buyers can leave. Hold it fixed at "absent." Loyalty can still land either way, since convenience alone pulls people over. Both checks pass, so the axes stay separate. I define a landing on loyalty as: by six months after opening, most of the regulars still buy knots weekly.

**Q5. Which tiles survive?**

Four combinations, against a budget of seven (three "knowable if" entries plus four "would help to know" items, from Q2's last two piles).

Triage with one step of lookahead:

- Loyal, no rival roll: likely. The knot keeps its place and the newcomer competes on bread.
- Loyal, rival roll: likely. Regulars hold while walk-ins split.
- Drift, no rival roll: likely. Convenience pulls even without a matching product.
- Drift, rival roll: likely. Each pressure reinforces the other.

Stress inside each tile: a rival roll pushes loyalty toward drift, yet "loyal" is defined by regulars, who are the customers a rival roll moves least, so the tile holds. The oven cap of 180 is a constraint on every tile and kills none, because a cap limits upside and does not make any landing impossible. No tile is killed, and none is inconsistent.

The four tiles, each with its story:

- **Steady queue** (loyal, no rival roll). Regulars keep coming for knots and the supermarket takes some bread. The bakery becomes more of a knot shop than a bakery.
- **Two knots** (loyal, rival roll). The supermarket sells a cheaper cinnamon roll and casual buyers try it. The regulars stay because they are buying the knot and the two people who make it.
- **Bread drain** (drift, no rival roll). The knot has no rival but the bread, pastry and coffee do. Knot buyers add a loaf and a coffee to their groceries across the street, and the attach sales fade.
- **Cold morning** (drift, rival roll). Both pressures land. Regulars cross the street for convenience and find a passable roll waiting there.

**Q6. Does every plausible future land in exactly one tile?**

Every future I can build lands on one side of "regulars stay or drift" and one side of "rival roll or none". Nothing fits no tile, and nothing fits two. A future in which the supermarket adds a roll only after six months lands where the roll was absent at the six-month mark; the definition of the axis settles it. Clean fit.

**Q7. Inside each tile, what follows from what?**

*Steady queue.* The supermarket's cheaper bread leads to a dip in bread and pastry sales. Regulars staying enables the knot to keep its 40% of takings. The 180-knot ceiling blocks any growth in knot sales beyond that.

*Two knots.* The rival roll leads to a price comparison for walk-ins. Regulars staying enables the bakery to lean on craft and relationship. The cap blocks growth.

*Bread drain.* Drift leads to fewer attach sales, meaning fewer loaves and coffees per knot buyer. The fall in the 60% of takings that is not knots leads to a draw on savings. The five-month runway limits how long the bakery can wait.

*Cold morning.* The rival roll and drift together lead to a fall in the knot's share as well as everything else. Falling takings lead to a draw on savings, with the runway again setting the clock.

**Merging.** Two consequences arrive in every tile:

- Bread, pastry and coffee, the 60% of takings that are not knots, face a cheaper competitor whichever future arrives. Reached in four of four tiles, so near-inevitable.
- Knot income cannot grow to offset losses, because 180 a morning is the ceiling under the current oven. Reached in four of four tiles. Any offset has to come from elsewhere: price per knot, another product, or more oven, and the cost of more oven is a fact I do not hold. I carry it as a named assumption: the owners have not priced a second oven or an extra bake slot. If that price turns out to be large, the "expand the oven" move below scores worse.

**Loop marked, not traced.** In Bread drain and Cold morning, fewer visitors make a thinner crowd at the counter, and a thinner crowd draws fewer walk-ins, which leads to fewer visitors. That loop feeds on itself. It trails the recommendation as an open item for a systems-thinking pass.

**Q8. What would I see first if an axis started to resolve?**

- **Loyalty.** Knots sold on the same weekday, compared with the month before opening, in the first three weeks after opening. A drop of more than about a fifth by week three (a threshold to adjust, not a measured figure) points to drift; a steady count points to loyalty. It is *observable* (the till and the tray show it), *early* (weeks before the five-month runway matters), and *discriminating* (drift predicts the drop, loyalty predicts its absence).
- **The rival roll.** Whether a cinnamon roll sits on the chain's counter, checked at the chain's other bakery counters and at the opening-week counter across the street. It is *observable* (anyone can look), *early* (some of it arrives before opening), and *discriminating* (the roll is there or it is not).

**Q9. Which move survives every tile?**

The owners gave no strategies, so I derive three from the tiles:

- **Hold course:** change nothing, sell knots as before.
- **Expand the oven:** buy a second oven or add a bake slot, to lift the 180 ceiling.
- **Knot club, trimmed counter, gated:** before opening day, offer regulars a standing weekly knot order collected at opening, so the bakery holds a floor of known demand. Trim the bread and pastry lines that overlap with what the supermarket will sell cheaper, keeping those that pair with a knot. Buy nothing large until the week-three watchpoint has spoken.

Two probes sharpen the scores.

*Backcast from the tile the owners want,* Steady queue: regulars must keep the weekly habit, the knot must stay the reason for the walk, and the bakery must not lose its bread margin faster than the knot covers it. The first two hold with no action; the third needs the counter trimmed.

*Premortem on Cold morning,* the tile they fear. Assume it happened. What made it inevitable? The regulars were tied to the bakery by proximity alone, so nothing held them once the street changed. The drift was invisible until savings were half spent. The 60% of the counter that subsidized the shop had no defense against a cheaper competitor. Each cause maps to a piece of the third move: standing orders tie regulars, the week-three watchpoint makes the drift visible, and trimming the counter cuts the exposed line.

Scores appear in the table below. The third move survives every tile. The best case for it is Two knots, where standing orders lock in the regulars against a cheaper roll.

The score for expanding the oven rests on the named assumption above, that the cost fits inside the runway. Hold course dies in the two drift tiles because takings fall with nothing done inside a five-month clock.

One move survives everywhere, so Q10 does not fire. Refined once against its weakest tile, Cold morning: the standing orders only lock in the regulars who sign up, and in that tile the regulars are the ones drifting. The floor they give is real and small.

## Deliver

Your bakery's next year splits on two things nobody can look up today: whether the people who come for your knots keep coming once a supermarket sits across the street, and whether that supermarket sells a cinnamon roll of its own.

In *Steady queue*, the regulars stay and the supermarket has no roll. Bread and coffee slip toward the cheaper aisle, and the knot carries the till. You become a knot shop that also sells bread. In *Two knots*, regulars still stay, but a cheaper roll sits across the street and the walk-in trade splits; the regulars hold because they are buying yours, from the two of you. *Bread drain* is the quiet loss: no rival roll, but knot buyers pick up a loaf and a coffee with their groceries, the attach sales fade, and savings begin to move. *Cold morning* is the harshest world. Regulars cross the street for convenience, find a passable roll there, and the counter thins from both ends while the five months of savings run.

| Tile | Landings | Hold course | Expand the oven | Knot club, trimmed counter |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Steady queue | regulars stay, no rival roll | survives | thrives | survives |
| Two knots | regulars stay, rival roll | survives | survives | thrives |
| Bread drain | regulars drift, no rival roll | dies | dies | survives |
| Cold morning | regulars drift, rival roll | dies | dies | survives |

Two things hold in every telling. Bread, pastry and coffee, the 60% of takings that are not knots, face a cheaper competitor whichever future arrives, so treat them as exposed now. And knot income tops out at 180 a morning, so the offset for any loss has to come from somewhere other than more knots on the current oven.

Two hinges tell you where you are. By week three after opening, compare knots sold with the same weekdays before opening: a drop of more than about a fifth means drift, so trim the counter harder and lean on the standing orders; a steady count means the knot is holding, so leave the counter be. And look at the chain's counters, at its other stores and at opening week: a cinnamon roll on the counter means craft is your edge, so move the story on your shelf from "famous knots" to "made here by us".

Recommendation: start a knot club before opening day, trim the bread lines the supermarket will undercut, and buy nothing large until week three has spoken. This survives all four worlds, so it is robust. It runs weakest in *Cold morning*, where the standing orders lock in only regulars who sign up and the drifting ones are the very regulars you need. The floor is small there, and the clock is your five months. That tile deserves a walk of its own, entered with both landings taken as known, so the plan for it exists before you need it. The lease is a later decision: it renews in about 14 months, and by then you will have about ten months of data on which tile you are in, so decide the option with that data and not before.

Open item for a systems-thinking pass: the loop in which a thinner crowd draws fewer walk-ins, which thins the crowd again.
