# question-the-question: cat on the keyboard

## Prompt

/question-the-question How do I get my cat to stop sitting on my keyboard?

## Run

### 1. Pause the inquiry

Stopped. The line of inquiry as it stood: "How do I get my cat to stop sitting on my keyboard?" Nothing has been tried yet, so there is no path to abandon, only a question to hold still and look at.

### 2. Ask why this question

**Why is this the question we are asking?**

Because the cat on the keyboard is the visible symptom. It interrupts work, it puts stray characters into documents, and it is the thing that happens at the desk. The question is phrased around the behavior the person wants gone: "stop sitting."

Read closely, the question carries a frame. It treats the sitting as the problem and the cat as the party who must change. The verb is "get my cat to", so the answer it invites is a technique applied to the cat: deterrents, training, blocking.

**What question should we be asking instead?**

Candidates, each aimed at a different target:

- "What is the cat getting from the keyboard, and can it get that somewhere else?" Aims at the cat's motive. The keyboard may be warm, may be where the person's attention goes, or may be the highest-traffic spot in the room.
- "What do I do when the cat sits there, and what has that taught it?" Aims at the loop between the two of them. If the person responds with attention every time, sitting may be an effective way to get attention. This is a hypothesis; nothing in the question says whether it holds here.
- "How do I protect my work from a cat on the keyboard?" Aims at the damage, not the behavior. Locking the screen or keeping a spare surface might solve the person's real problem without any change in the cat.
- "Do I want the cat gone from the desk, or only off the keyboard?" Aims at the person's own aim. Some people want the cat near them and only the keys clear.

The last one changes what counts as success, so it comes first among the four in weight. It is also the one only the person can answer.

### 3. Probe its assumptions

What must be true for "how do I get my cat to stop sitting on my keyboard?" to be the right question?

1. **The sitting is the problem.** It holds if the harm is the sitting itself. It fails if the harm is lost work, stray input, or a broken routine, since those can be handled without touching the cat's behavior.
2. **The cat can be changed, and the person is the one to change it.** Plausible for many cats. It also assumes that a cat who is stopped from sitting there will not simply move to the next-best spot, such as the desk in front of the screen or the person's arms. If it does, the problem moved and did not go away.
3. **The keyboard is what the cat wants.** It holds if the cat is drawn to the keys, their warmth, or their height. It fails if the cat wants the person, and the keyboard is only where the person's hands and eyes are. Under that reading, removing the keyboard as a seat leaves the wanting unmet.
4. **"Stop" means never.** It holds if any sitting counts as failure. It fails if the person would accept a cat who sits nearby, or who sits on the keyboard only when the person is away. The word hides a boundary the person has not drawn.
5. **The cat's sitting is a single behavior with a single cause.** It may not be. A cat who sits on a keyboard at a work desk during the day and one who does so while the person sleeps or is out could be doing the same thing for different reasons.
6. **The person has a choice about what to give the cat instead.** If they cannot offer an alternative that competes with the keyboard, an answer built on redirection has nothing to redirect toward.

Unverified, and worth checking: assumption 3 is the one carrying the most weight. If the cat wants attention, every technique that only blocks the keyboard is at war with the motive.

### 4. Check for drift

The core concern, stated plainly: the person wants to work at their computer without a cat in the way.

The question has drifted from that in one place. "Stop sitting" moved the target from the person's work to the cat's posture. The original concern says nothing about the cat's behavior. It is about the person being able to type. The question narrowed the goal into one particular means, changing what the cat does, before other means were on the table.

The drift is small and natural. It is also the kind that closes off options quietly. Once the question is "how do I stop the cat," the answers that come back are all about the cat, and nobody asks whether the desk, the schedule, or the setup could change.

### 5. Sharpen or reframe

The question is not well-aimed as posed. It is close, but it aims at the cat's posture where the concern is the person's work. So this step reframes and redirects, then sharpens the reframed question so it can be acted on.

**Reframed question.** "What does my cat get from sitting on my keyboard, and how can I keep my work safe and my cat close, or apart, in a way I actually want?"

That splits into three questions the person can take one at a time:

1. **What does the cat get from it?** Sharpened: over the next several days, note when the sitting happens (time of day, whether the person is typing or away, what happened just before) and what the person does in response. A concrete example of what to look for: does the cat arrive right after the person sits down, or only after a long stretch of typing? The first points to wanting attention or a place near the person. The second points to a change in what the person is doing.
2. **What counts as success?** Sharpened: pick a boundary in one sentence. Examples of clear boundaries: "the cat is never on the keys while I work"; "the cat can be on the desk but not the keyboard"; "the cat can be on my lap but not the desk." Each implies a different fix. Without one, "stop" cannot be measured.
3. **What can the cat have instead?** Sharpened: name a specific alternative that fits the boundary: a bed or blanket at the edge of the desk, a spot beside the screen, a scheduled play session before work. Name it before trying anything, so the change is an offer as well as a block.

**What stays from the original.** The person's wish to type undisturbed is legitimate and stays as the aim. The reframing keeps it and widens the routes to it.

**Where the reframing could be wrong.** If the cat sits on the keyboard purely for warmth and has no interest in the person, question 1 will return nothing about attention, and a warm alternative spot may resolve it at once. If the person actually wants the cat off the desk entirely, question 2 will surface that, and the reframing to "close, or apart" is too soft.
