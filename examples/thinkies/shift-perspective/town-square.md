# shift-perspective: livening up the town square

- skill: shift-perspective, from commit 0c9258f
- subject: The town square, which the council wants to "liven up". See it as a pigeon, as the street-sweeper who starts at 5 a.m., and as the owner of the café on its north side.
- kind: single run

## Prompt

/shift-perspective The town square, which the council wants to "liven up". See it as a pigeon, as the street-sweeper who starts at 5 a.m., and as the owner of the café on its north side.

## Run

### 1. The current frame

The frame the subject arrives in is the council's. It treats the square as a stage that is currently underperforming: "liven up" assumes the square is dull, that dullness is a property of the square, and that the remedy is to add something (events, stalls, lighting, art, a fountain). It sees the square from above, as a plan, on a budget cycle, at midday.

Three frames contrast with it. They were named in the request, so no others were chosen. They differ by species, hour of day, and stake: a pigeon (no stake in the plan, total stake in the ground), a sweeper (works the square when nobody else is there, and pays for every addition with labor), and a café owner (earns from the square's crowd, and owns one edge of it).

### 2. The perspectives chosen

- The pigeon: a nonhuman resident, active all day, outside the council's decision-making.
- The street-sweeper: a municipal worker, present from 5 a.m., the one who receives the square's aftermath.
- The café owner: a private business on the north side, dependent on footfall, sun, and seating space.

### 3. Each inhabited

**The pigeon.**

I do not know the square is called a square. I know it as a surface with a pattern of food. I assume the ground stays open and flat, because I land and walk there, and nothing tall stands where I need to take off. I prioritize crumbs, which arrive from the café's terrace, from the bench where the man eats lunch at noon, and from the bin that overflows on market day. I protect my ledge on the clock-tower side, my dry spot under the bandstand roof, and the low fountain rim where the water stands still enough to drink.

A "livelier" square is good news to me in one case only: more people eating outside. Stalls with food are a feast. Banners and hanging lights are a question mark. A fenced flower bed is a loss of ground. Loud amplified music makes me leave, and I come back when it stops, as I always have. I am also the reason the sweeper's morning is what it is, and I know it.

**The street-sweeper.**

I start at 5 a.m., when the square is at its emptiest and I can see it whole. I assume the square is a surface I must clear before the day's first person crosses it, in a fixed number of hours, with a cart and a broom and a route I have refined over years. I prioritize the route: the sequence that lets me finish before the café puts out its chairs. I protect my time and my back.

What I know is what the day left behind. I know which corner collects wind-blown paper, which bench leaves bottle caps, where the pigeons roost and what is underneath. I know that every event leaves a debris pattern, and that the debris is what an event actually costs. A market leaves crates and wet cardboard. A concert leaves cups across the whole surface. A light show leaves nothing. Cobbles between planters are slow to sweep; a flat open surface is fast. "Liven up" reaches me as: more to clean, by the same hour, with the same crew, unless somebody tells me otherwise.

**The café owner.**

I sit on the north side, which is the sunny side, and that is not an accident of the map. It is my business. I assume the square's crowd is my customer base and that my terrace depends on sight lines, light, and room for tables. I prioritize footfall between eight and six, and above all the two hours when people choose where to sit. I protect my terrace license, my sun, and the view of the square that people pay for with a coffee.

Livening up the square is what I have wanted for years, and it is also what could hurt me. An event that draws a crowd fills my tables, so I am for it. A stage placed in front of my terrace, a market stall selling coffee, or a tall installation that shades my tables costs me more than any crowd returns. I would rather the council ask me where the stage goes than tell me after the drawing is done. I also know that an event day is a hard day for me: deliveries blocked, staff short, and the square's noise in my customers' ears.

### 4. The blind spots

What each frame sees that the others miss:

- The pigeon sees the square as a food surface and knows precisely where food comes from. It misses money, schedules, and law. It does not see that the crumbs it counts on exist because of the café's terrace and would vanish if the square emptied.
- The sweeper sees the square as a cleanup problem and knows the true cost of each event. It misses the reason people come, and does not see that the café's income helps pay for the square's upkeep through the license fee.
- The café owner sees the square as a customer supply. The owner misses the quiet hours completely, because the owner is asleep or absent at 5 a.m., and misses the fact that the square's hardest daily work is done before the first customer sits.
- The council's frame, the starting one, sees the square as a stage and misses all three: the animal that lives on the ground, the labor that resets the ground each morning, and the business whose income depends on a particular edge of it.

None of the three sees the square as a place where nothing needs to happen. The council's word "liven up" hides a claim that stillness is a defect. Only the pigeon comes near disputing it, because for the pigeon the square is lively already.

### 5. The clashes

These are genuine conflicts, where satisfying one frame costs another.

- **Open ground versus additions.** The pigeon and the sweeper both want the surface flat and uncluttered, the pigeon to land and the sweeper to work fast. The council's remedy, adding fixtures, takes ground from both.
- **Crowd versus cleanup.** The café owner wants a bigger crowd. The sweeper receives that crowd's waste at 5 a.m. with the same crew. More footfall for one is more labor for the other.
- **Food on the ground.** The café owner's terrace and event stalls feed the pigeon, and the pigeon's droppings and scavenging add to the sweeper's route and to the owner's tables. The same crumbs are supply for one, cost for another, and nuisance for a third.
- **Position on the square.** The owner wants activity on the north side. Placed in front of the terrace, it blocks the owner's own view; placed elsewhere, it may pull footfall away.

Two apparent clashes turn out not to be. The pigeon and the café owner do not conflict over crowds: both gain from people eating outdoors. The sweeper and the council do not conflict over improvement itself, only over who is told and when.

### 6. The insight no single frame produced

"Livening up" the square is a decision about who carries the cost of liveliness, and no version of the plan has said so.

The council's frame counts the benefit, a livelier square. The café owner counts the benefit and the position. The pigeon counts the crumbs. Only the sweeper counts the residue, and the sweeper is the one whose work runs before, and outside, any consultation. Set beside each other, the three frames show that each kind of liveliness has a different debris profile, and the profile decides who pays. A market pays in cardboard and cups, a concert pays in cups across the whole surface, a light show pays in almost nothing, and a fountain or planter takes ground that both the pigeon and the sweeper depend on.

Two concrete consequences follow, and neither appears from the council's seat.

1. Before choosing what to add, ask the sweeper what each option leaves behind and how long it takes to clear. That is a cost the plan can price before it commits, and the sweeper is the only person who can price it.
2. The square already runs on a fragile arrangement: the café's crowd produces the crumbs that keep the pigeons, and the pigeons and the crowd together produce the morning's work. Any addition that changes one of the three flows changes the other two. Decide where the additions go with that loop in view, and put the north side last.

An unresolved question the run leaves open: whether the square is dull at all, and to whom. The council says so. No one inside the square, from the pigeon to the owner, said it.
