# find-leverage: dishes in a shared flat of four

- skill: find-leverage, from commit 0c9258f
- subject: A shared flat of four where the dishes never get done. A rota was tried twice and died within a week each time; the sink fills, people buy paper plates, the paper plates run out, the sink fills again.
- kind: single run

## Prompt

/find-leverage A shared flat of four where the dishes never get done. A rota was tried twice and died within a week each time; the sink fills, people buy paper plates, the paper plates run out, the sink fills again.

## Run

### 1. Trace the feedback loops

Four loops run through the flat. Two reinforce and two balance.

- **The full-sink loop (reinforcing).** The sink fills, so washing becomes a large job, so each person defers, so the sink fills further. What flows around it is dirty dishes and the size of the job a person imagines when they look at the sink.
- **The free-rider loop (reinforcing).** One person leaves a dish. Another sees it, decides that washing their own would be the act of a sucker, and leaves theirs. What flows around it is a belief about what the other three are doing. Because a dish in a pile has no visible owner, the belief cannot be checked, and it gets read in the worst direction.
- **The paper-plate loop (balancing, and it fails on a delay).** The sink is full and clean crockery has run out, so someone buys paper plates, which removes the pressure to wash. The pile stays. The plates run out, and the pressure returns at full strength. This loop pushes toward a clean kitchen and never gets there, because it relieves the need to wash and leaves the washing undone. What flows around it is pressure and cash, and the plates cost the flat something each cycle.
- **The rota loop (balancing, and it died twice).** A rota assigns each day to a person. The first missed day goes unenforced, so the next person on the rota sees the duty as optional, so more days are missed, and by the end of the week nobody is following it. What flows around it is a promise, and the flat has no way to make a broken promise cost anything.

### 2. Locate bottlenecks

- **The moment of washing.** All the work is scheduled for a time the person does not choose. A rota picks Tuesday; hunger and dishes both arrive on other days.
- **The full sink as a start condition.** A person can wash a plate into a clear sink in under a minute. Into a full sink, they must first clear the pile, and that is where most people stop. The bottleneck is the first dish, and each later dish is easy.
- **Drying and storage space.** A small rack is full after a few plates. If washing a whole pile is possible only when there is somewhere to put it, the rack gates the work. This is a guess about a typical flat and the flat could confirm or deny it.
- **Attribution.** A pile of eight items cannot be traced to the person who left them, so no one can address a person about them. The flat has nobody to ask.

### 3. Find amplification points

- **The dirty dish at the moment of use.** A dish washed within minutes takes seconds, and a washed dish is one fewer thing the pile can attract. Each dish handled at that point removes work from every later moment.
- **The state of the sink.** All four residents see it several times a day. A clear sink signals that the others wash, and a full one signals that they do not. This single visible state feeds the free-rider loop, so it moves what all four believe.
- **Who holds the shortage.** When clean crockery runs out, the whole flat feels it, so the person who caused it and the three who did not are equally short of plates. That is why the paper-plate purchase looks reasonable to everyone. The shortage is where the consequence of one person's act could land on one person, and it lands on all four.

### 4. Spot compounding

- **Time makes each dish harder.** Food that dries takes longer to remove. A pile costs more than the sum of its dishes, and the cost goes up while it waits.
- **Failed rotas make the next rota harder.** Two collapsed rotas have left the flat with a shared history: proposing a rota now costs credibility as well as effort. A third attempt of the same form starts from a lower base.
- **A clear sink compounds the other way.** A kitchen left empty after one careful day holds to that state more easily on the next. The pile and the clear sink both grow by the same mechanism, and only the starting point differs.
- **Paper plate waste.** Each cycle spends money and produces rubbish, and none of it moves the flat toward a clean kitchen.

### 5. Identify root causes

Three causes produce most of the symptoms above.

1. **A dish in the sink belongs to nobody.** Ownership stops at the moment the dish leaves the hand, which produces the free-rider loop, the failed rotas (nobody can be held to account for a pile), and the belief that the others do not wash.
2. **The consequence of leaving a dish lands on the group.** Crockery is shared, so a shortage is shared, so the person who leaves a dish pays a quarter of the cost. The paper plates spread that cost even further.
3. **The washing schedule ignores when dishes are made.** A rota assigns work by calendar day, and dishes are made at meals. On a rota day, a person washes the mess of the other three, which feels like a penalty and gives them a reason to drop out.

Cause 1 and cause 2 are one fact seen from two sides: the flat's crockery and its clean-up have no link to a person.

### 6. Evaluate leverage ratio

Candidates, each judged by how much it shifts and what effort keeps it going.

| Candidate | What it shifts | Effort to maintain | Ratio |
|---|---|---|---|
| A third rota | Schedule (cause 3) | Daily attention and enforcement from four people; twice already lost | Low |
| Nagging, a chore chart, fines | Pressure on the pile | Constant, and it falls on whoever nags | Low |
| More paper plates, or a bigger stock of shared crockery | The symptom (a shortage) | Money each cycle; makes cause 2 worse by delaying the shortage | Negative |
| A dishwasher | Removes the labour of washing | Capital cost, space, and loading and unloading still need owners, so causes 1 and 2 return in a new form | Medium, conditional on the flat having one |
| Wash your own dish at the point of use, as a norm | The moment of washing (causes 1 and 3) | Depends on habit in four people; no mechanism holds it up when one slips | Medium |
| Personal crockery, with no shared stock | Ownership and consequence (causes 1 and 2) | A single setup; after that the arrangement runs by itself | High |

The last candidate scores highest because the structure does the work of the rota. A rota needs four people to remember, and personal crockery needs each person to want a clean plate at their next meal, which they already do.

### 7. Name the intervention

**Give each resident their own small set of crockery, and put every other plate, bowl, and mug away.** One plate, one bowl, one mug, and one set of cutlery each, marked or kept distinguishable by colour, and no shared spare stock. Pots, pans, and serving dishes belong to whoever cooked with them.

Why this one point moves the flat:

- **Ownership travels with the dish.** A dirty plate in the sink has a name on it in effect. Cause 1 goes away, and with it the belief that the others do not wash, because each person can check.
- **The shortage lands on the person who made it.** Someone who leaves their plate has no plate at their next meal, and no one else is short. The paper-plate purchase stops making sense, because nobody else has a reason to buy them. Cause 2 goes away.
- **The washing moves to the moment of need.** A person washes when they are hungry, which is when their own dish is needed. Cause 3 goes away without a schedule.
- **It needs no continuing effort.** After the setup, the incentive is built into the crockery and nobody has to remember anything.

What the flat must do once to make it work: clear and wash the existing pile in one sitting, and box the spare crockery out of reach (a full cupboard restarts the free-rider loop, because a spare plate hides the shortage). The one-time cost is an afternoon and it is the only bottleneck the intervention leaves in place.

Where it may fail, and what to watch:

- **Cooking pots.** Shared cooking equipment has the same gap as the old plates. The rule that the cook washes the pot is a norm, and norms are what failed here twice. A pot left in the sink is the first sign the pile is returning. If it happens, a fix in the same spirit is to make each pot belong to whoever last cooked with it, and to leave it on their shelf rather than in the sink.
- **Guests and visitors.** They will need something. Paper plates kept for them alone, and stored where residents will not use them, is a small exception the arrangement can absorb.
- **A resident who accepts having no plate.** They may eat from the pan or buy takeaway, and the pile moves to packaging. This is a guess and the flat would learn it within a fortnight.
