**Subject:** The bedside alarm clock, a single-purpose timepiece that makes a sound at a time the sleeper chose, examined against everything else that gets a sleeper awake at a chosen time.

1. What job does the bedside alarm clock do, and at what level of detail does it sit?

   It ends a sleeper's sleep at a moment the sleeper picked the night before, by putting a signal in front of the sleeper's senses that is strong enough to wake them. The grain is the whole waking act: a thing or agent that, on its own, picks up a chosen time and turns it into a waking sleeper. Parts of that act (a buzzer, a timer chip, a snooze button) sit below the grain. Systems that contain it (a morning routine, a sleep schedule) sit above.

2. What else gets a sleeper awake at a chosen time, at the level of a whole thing or agent that does the whole job, including things from outside the world of gadgets?

   Devices the sleeper sets:
   - Phone alarm app.
   - Smart speaker alarm, set by voice.
   - Radio alarm, which starts a broadcast.
   - Wind-up bell clock.
   - Sunrise lamp, which brightens over the half hour before the set time.
   - Vibrating wristband or watch.
   - Vibrating pad under the pillow or mattress, made for sleepers who cannot hear an alarm.
   - Scheduled coffee maker, and the niche scent-releasing alarm clock.
   - Thermostat programmed to warm the bedroom before the set time.

   Another party who keeps the time:
   - Hotel wake-up call.
   - Roommate, partner, or parent who says they will wake you.
   - The knocker-upper, a paid person who once tapped sleepers' windows with a pole until they answered.
   - A church bell, muezzin call, or factory whistle, one signal for a whole district.
   - A night nurse on rounds.

   No time is set; the world keeps it:
   - Birdsong or a rooster.
   - Dawn light through an uncurtained window.
   - A pet whose hunger is timed by its own body.
   - Bladder pressure after drinking water at bedtime, a folk method for waking at a chosen hour.
   - The sleeper's own intention to wake at a certain hour, a self-awakening some people report.

3. What single property most separates each of those peers from the bedside alarm clock?

   - Phone alarm app: it lives in the device that holds everything else the sleeper wants, so dismissing it lands the sleeper in the feed.
   - Smart speaker: it is set by voice and can depend on a network and a cloud account.
   - Radio alarm: the waking signal is a programme someone else broadcasts.
   - Wind-up bell clock: it needs no power and no network, and stops when the spring runs down.
   - Sunrise lamp: it wakes through light that rises gradually, with no sudden start.
   - Vibrating wristband or watch: it wakes only the wearer and makes no sound in the room.
   - Vibrating pad: it wakes through the body of a sleeper who cannot hear.
   - Scheduled coffee maker or scent clock: it puts smell in the room and cannot force a sleeper up.
   - Thermostat warm-up: it changes the room's warmth and carries no signal meant as a wake-up.
   - Hotel wake-up call: a stranger's system places a call, and the phone must be answered.
   - Roommate, partner, or parent: a person can see whether you actually got up and escalate.
   - Knocker-upper: the waker stays until the sleeper shows they are awake.
   - Church bell, muezzin call, or factory whistle: one signal serves many sleepers and no sleeper picks the hour.
   - Night nurse on rounds: the waking is a side effect of another duty and follows a care schedule, not the sleeper's wish.
   - Birdsong or rooster: its hour moves with the sun and the season.
   - Dawn light: it is free and unset, and drifts by season and by the window's direction.
   - Pet: it wakes the sleeper by hunger, not by clock.
   - Bladder after bedtime water: it uses the sleeper's own body, and its timing varies with what was drunk.
   - Self-awakening intention: it needs nothing outside the sleeper, and it fails without warning.

4. How do the peers arrange along the one or two differences that separate them most?

   Two differences separate them most. The first is who keeps the time: a device the sleeper set, another party, or the world with no time set. The second is which sense carries the signal: sound, light, touch (including warmth and pressure), or smell.

   |                                   | Sound                                                             | Light                                  | Touch (including warmth, pressure)                                          | Smell                                   |
   | --------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------- |
   | Device the sleeper set            | Bedside alarm clock, phone, smart speaker, radio alarm, wind-up   | Sunrise lamp                           | Vibrating wristband, vibrating pad, thermostat warm-up                      | Scheduled coffee maker, scent clock     |
   | Another party keeps the time      | Hotel call, roommate's voice, knocker-upper, church bell, whistle | Parent switching on the light          | Roommate's shake, knocker-upper's tap, night nurse's hand                   | Someone cooking breakfast               |
   | The world keeps it, no time set   | Birdsong, rooster, pet's cry                                      | Dawn light                             | Bladder pressure, cold or sun-warmed air                                    | Empty (see 5)                           |

   The sleeper's own intention to wake has no sense channel, so it sits outside the grid. It is the one peer that varies neither by device nor by party.

5. Which positions in that arrangement does nothing occupy, and is each empty by accident or for a reason?

   Two positions stand empty.

   - World keeps the time, smell carries the signal. It is empty for a reason: no natural scent turns up at a fixed hour, so smell carries no clock of its own. The smells that do wake people, cooking or a bakery down the street, come from another party's schedule.
   - Another party keeps the time, touch is scheduled through a device for many sleepers at once. It is empty for a reason: touch needs contact with one body at a time, so it cannot reach a district the way a bell or a whistle does. The touch peers that exist are one person at one bedside.

   One near-empty position is worth naming, and this run cannot tell whether it is empty by accident or for a reason. The cell for a device that a different party sets on the sleeper's behalf, a parent setting a child's alarm from another room, holds a real practice but sits inside the rows above, because the grid asks who keeps the time and both answers fit. This run did not test which reading the grid should take.

6. Where does the bedside alarm clock sit among its peers, and which peers are nearest?

   It sits at the centre of the densest cell: the sleeper sets a device, and sound carries the signal. Its nearest neighbours share that whole cell: the phone alarm app, the radio alarm, the wind-up bell clock, and the smart speaker. The plain bedside clock differs from all four by being single-purpose, a property none of its cell-mates shares, since each also does something else.

   Its next nearest peers each share one coordinate. Sunrise lamps, vibrating wristbands, and scheduled coffee makers share who keeps the time and change the sense. Hotel wake-up calls, the knocker-upper's pole, and church bells share the sound and change who keeps the time.

   The far corner from the subject is dawn light and birdsong: the world keeps the time, nothing is set, and the sleeper owns none of it.
