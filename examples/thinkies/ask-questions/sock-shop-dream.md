# ask-questions: what to ask a friend who wants a sock-only shop

- skill: ask-questions, from commit 0c9258f
- subject: A friend says "I think I want to quit my job and open a sock-only shop." What should I ask them next?
- kind: single run

## Prompt

/ask-questions A friend says "I think I want to quit my job and open a sock-only shop." What should I ask them next?

## Run

No record of the exchange so far was supplied, so I am working from the one sentence the friend said and nothing else. If the friend has said more, the choice below may change.

**Driving question:** What pulled my friend toward this, and how settled is it, so that I can tell whether to encourage or caution?

**The next move:** one question, said warmly and then left alone.

> What got you thinking about it?

**Why this one**

- Relevance: any full answer partly answers the driving question. "My boss" points to an escape, "I saw a shop like it once" points to a pull, "I have been planning it for two years" points to a settled plan, and "since last night" points to a mood. Those four readings decide whether you encourage or caution.
- Discrimination: each of those answers sends the next question somewhere different, so the question earns its turn.
- Presupposition: it assumes only that something led the friend to the thought, and the friend's own words ("I think I want to") establish that. It does not assume the friend is unhappy at work, has a plan, or wants a verdict.
- Clarity laws: one idea; plain words the friend could have used; no premise smuggled in; a real question with no answer inside it.

**What I weighed and cut**

- "Have you thought about the money?" and "Are you sure?" both fail the fourth law. They are caution wearing a question mark, they tell the friend what you fear, and they stand on an unestablished premise, that the friend has not thought about it.
- "What's wrong with your job right now?" fails the third law. The friend named no complaint; it plants one.
- "Why socks?" is relevant and nearly passes. It collapses less than the pick, since it asks about the product and leaves the pull toward leaving unexplored, and spoken flatly it can sound like disbelief.
- "On a scale of one to ten, how much do you want this?" waits. It presupposes the friend is ready to be graded, which is not yet established. It becomes a candidate once the story is told.

**The moves that aren't questions**

- Silence loses. The friend has already spoken and would be left holding the announcement. A pause after the question is the silence that matters, and the reply should end once it is asked.
- A restatement ("so you're thinking of leaving your job for a shop that sells only socks") mostly echoes what was said. Its useful part is the "I think": the friend hedged, and a restatement could name that. It loses to the question because the question invites the same hedge to unfold, and it does not risk putting words in the friend's mouth.
- A plain statement of your own state ("I'm not sure yet what to say, and I want to hear more") is worth saying, since a friend's trust and a shared decision ride on this. It goes in front of the question as your reason for asking. It does not replace the question.

**A tension I settled.** The friend hedged, and a hesitant speaker gets a small, easy factual question first, such as "How long has this been on your mind?". One hedge is thin evidence of hesitancy, and the chosen question is itself an easy on-ramp: it asks for a story about the friend's own thought and requires no defense. I chose it because it rules out the most. If the reply comes back short or guarded, the small factual question is the fallback.

**What the ladder holds next**, each rung grown only from what the answer licenses:

1. If the answer names a push, such as a job the friend wants out of, ask what the friend would want from the next stretch of work. The shop is then one candidate among several.
2. If it names a pull, ask what the friend pictures on an ordinary day in the shop.
3. If it names a plan, ask what the friend has already done toward it.
4. If it names a mood, ask what changed since it arrived.
5. Close a long conversation open: "What haven't I asked that I should have?"

**What I grant, and would choose to extend now:** that the friend means the sentence at least partly, that the friend wants to be heard rather than steered, and that your own uncertainty is allowed to show. The first two could be overturned by the friend's next answer, and if so the rungs above get re-tested. The third dissolves the inquiry if doubted, so it stays.

**How this closes.** The inquiry serves your decision to encourage or caution, so an earned exit is licensed. Once the friend's story shows a push or a pull and how settled it is, a direct statement of what you heard serves better than more questions. If the friend does not know yet, an honest non-answer, stated as such, is a fine place to stop. Questioning past either point would be the failure.
