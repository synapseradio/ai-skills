# consider-alternatives: the great sock loss

- skill: consider-alternatives, from commit 0c9258f
- subject: Every sock in the house lost its partner in the same week. What explains it?
- kind: single run

## Prompt

/consider-alternatives Every sock in the house lost its partner in the same week. What explains it?

## Run

### 1. The current explanation

The default explanation for a missing sock is that the dryer or washer swallowed it. Socks go in as pairs and come out as singles, so the machine is blamed.

The evidence for it is real but thin:

- Socks pass through the washer and dryer more often than any other item, so a machine is the likeliest place for a loss.
- Missing socks are a familiar household complaint, so the explanation arrives ready-made.

Two observations strain it. The loss hit every sock in the house, where a machine fault would usually take some. And it landed in one week, where a machine has been doing the same thing for months. Whatever is true must explain "all" and "one week", or it is at best a piece of the answer.

Working facts, held as stated: every sock now lacks its partner, and the loss falls inside one seven-day window. Unknown so far: whether "lost" means gone from the house or only not in the drawer, whether the count of single socks equals the count of pairs before, and whether anything else went missing.

### 2. Alternatives

Each alternative varies one thing: the mechanism, the scope of what happened, the timing, or the direction of cause.

1. **One event at the end of the machine.** Something happened to the dryer that week, such as a vent blockage, a torn drum seal, or a new gap behind the drum, and it now traps or pulls one sock of each pair. Mechanism: physical trap. Scope: every load.
2. **Everything was washed, then only half was put away.** A single big laundry push, for example after a trip or an illness, ran every sock through the wash. One person folded and put away half, and the other half sits in a basket, a bag, or a chair. Mechanism: sorting and storage, no loss. Scope: the whole house, because the push was whole-house.
3. **The pairing changed, not the socks.** A person cleared out old odd socks, or matched new ones, and re-paired by a different rule. Or a new sock brand or a new color scheme entered the mix, so that pairs that once matched no longer read as pairs. Mechanism: a change in what counts as a pair. Nothing left.
4. **A person or pet is moving socks.** A dog, a toddler, or a collector is carrying single socks to a nest or a hiding place. Timing: the behavior began that week, for instance with a new puppy, a teething stage, or a new game. Mechanism: theft by an agent in the house.
5. **One sock of each pair is worn out and was thrown away.** Holes went through at the same time because the socks were bought together and aged together. Someone binned the worn halves in one clean-out and left their still-good partners. Timing: a shared purchase date makes shared wear. Mechanism: partial disposal.
6. **The pairs were split across two places.** A move, a guest stay, or a shared laundry with another household means one sock of each pair is elsewhere, in a bag, a suitcase, or a friend's machine. Scope: everything that left the house in one trip. Timing: the trip was that week.
7. **A cause that combines two others.** A partial clean-out (5) happened in the same week as a whole-house wash (2). Half the loss is disposal and half is a basket nobody emptied, and each looks like the other from the drawer.
8. **Direction of cause reversed: the socks were not lost, the count was.** Someone began checking pairs that week, after a first missing sock made the topic salient. Socks that had always been odd were noticed for the first time. Nothing changed in the socks. Attention changed. This is also the reading where the "same week" is produced by the observer and not by the socks.
9. **Coincidence.** Sock loss has a steady background rate. A house full of socks will lose a few every week, and a person who counts singles once, in a bad week, sees a large number and calls it an event. Weak for "every sock", stronger for "a lot of socks", which is why the wording of the claim matters.

### 3. Each alternative made concrete

For each, the mechanism in a sentence and what it would predict if true.

1. **Trapped in the dryer.**
   - Works by: a gap or blockage collects fabric and holds one sock at a time.
   - Predicts: the trapped socks are in or behind the machine. The dryer runs hot, slow, or noisy. Other small items (washcloths, underwear) are also missing. Partners are found singly, one per pair, not in clumps by color.
2. **Not put away.**
   - Works by: one folding pass finished half the pile, and the other half stays in a container.
   - Predicts: a basket, bag, or chair holds a pile of loose socks. The singles in the drawer and the singles in the pile match each other. Nothing else in the same wash is unfolded, or only similar small items are.
3. **Re-paired by a new rule.**
   - Works by: the old pair rule (same style, same wear) stopped matching what is in the drawer.
   - Predicts: the total sock count is unchanged from a month ago. Someone recalls a re-sorting or a new purchase. Socks that "have no partner" look close to something else in the drawer.
4. **Moved by a person or pet.**
   - Works by: a small agent picks single socks and carries them.
   - Predicts: singles cluster in one place, such as under a bed, in a crate, or behind a couch. Chew marks or damp spots on some. A new pet, or a child at a new stage, in the household. The rate of loss continues after the week.
5. **Half of each pair thrown away.**
   - Works by: wear reached the same threshold for both socks, and a clean-out discarded only the holed ones.
   - Predicts: the remaining singles are the better-condition socks of their pairs. The trash or the ragbag holds the holed halves, if not yet collected. Someone remembers a clean-out. Only older, pair-bought socks are affected.
6. **Split across two places.**
   - Works by: one sock of each pair travelled and did not return.
   - Predicts: a bag or suitcase from that week holds many socks. The missing socks are the ones worn on the trip, not a random sample. Socks that stayed home kept their partners.
7. **Combined causes.**
   - Works by: two causes overlap and each accounts for part of the count.
   - Predicts: the singles split into two groups by condition or by location. Neither cause alone recovers all of them.
8. **The count changed, not the socks.**
   - Works by: a first noticed absence made a person start counting.
   - Predicts: the drawer looks the same as a month ago. Singles are older socks. Someone can name when they began checking pairs. Old odd socks, long tolerated, appear in the tally.
9. **Coincidence.**
   - Works by: ordinary loss adds up, and one count makes it look sudden.
   - Predicts: no cluster, no location, no cause found when searched. A rate of a few singles a week, over months, would reach the current total. A later count finds fewer singles than this one, or the same, not more.

### 4. Distinguishing tests

The tests below are ordered by how cheaply they cut the list. The first three cost minutes and settle most of it.

1. **Search the places a sock can sit.** Check a basket, bag, chair, dryer drum, dryer vent, under beds, and behind furniture. Result and what it rules:
   - A pile of loose socks: alternative 2 is likely. Compare it against the singles in the drawer; if they pair up, 2 is confirmed and 1, 5 and 9 are out for those socks.
   - A nest in one place: alternative 4.
   - Socks in or behind the dryer: alternative 1.
   - Nothing anywhere: 1 and 2 weaken; 3, 5, 6, 8 and 9 stay.
2. **Count.** Count the singles and count the pairs a month ago, using a photo or the shopping record if one exists.
   - Total unchanged, singles now visible: alternatives 3 and 8.
   - Total lower by half the pairs: alternatives 1, 4, 5, 6 (something left the drawer).
   - Total lower by a few: alternative 9.
3. **Read the leftovers.** Look at the condition and age of the singles.
   - Mostly the better half of each pair: alternative 5.
   - A random mix of condition: alternatives 1, 2, 4, 6, 9.
   - Mostly socks worn on one trip: alternative 6.
4. **Ask each person in the house one question: what did you do with socks that week?** This separates the events (clean-out, trip, big wash, re-sort) from the non-events (8, 9), and it names any unrecorded event that fits a combined cause, alternative 7.
5. **Run a test load.** Put ten marked socks, five pairs, through the dryer and count on exit. A loss now favors alternative 1. No loss over several loads makes 1 unlikely, and the dryer stays innocent for the rest of the list.
6. **Watch for continuation.** Set out a fresh pair and see whether it stays whole over a week. If the pair is still whole, whatever happened was an event and not a standing mechanism, which leans to 2, 5 and 6 and away from 1, 4 and 9. If the pair loses a sock, a standing mechanism is at work, which leans to 1 and 4.
7. **Check the other small items.** Missing washcloths, underwear, or gloves point at alternatives 1, 4, or 6 and away from 3, 5, 8, which act on socks only.

The likeliest starting point, given "every sock" and "one week", is a single event that touched the whole house at once. That favors 2 (a whole-house wash left half unpacked) and 6 (half the pairs travelled), with 1 in the running if the dryer is failing. Alternatives 8 and 9 explain "every sock" least well and stay on the list only until test 2 clears them. Test 1 is where to begin: it costs minutes and can finish the matter by itself.
