**Subject:** The single traffic cone standing on a quiet residential street for three weeks, placed by an unknown person

1. What crosses the edge of the single traffic cone on the quiet residential street: what feeds it, what takes what it produces, and what does it serve? What is the smallest system that holds all three?

   Feeds it: a person's decision to put it down, once, three weeks ago. Nothing has fed it since. The only continuing input is the attention of passers-by who see it. A cone needs no maintenance, so its silence about who placed it costs it nothing.

   Takes what it produces: a cone produces a signal, "something here needs care." The takers are the drivers, cyclists, and walkers who pass and adjust their path, and the neighbors who look at it from windows. Three weeks of steering around a spot, or of deciding to ignore it, is its output.

   What it serves: a purpose that has to be inferred, since nobody stated it. Candidates are a reserved parking spot, a marked hole or hazard, a dodge around a pothole, or a leftover from finished work. Each candidate is a claim about the street that nobody has confirmed.

   The smallest system holding all three: the street as a shared space, made of the people who use it, the informal rules for its use, and the party with authority over it (a neighborhood, a municipality). The cone sits inside that system as one of its signals.

2. What does that system ask of the cone that the cone cannot see from where it stands: which limits, rhythms, expectations, and competitors for the same resources does the system impose?

   Limits: a street signal only works while people read it as meaningful. A cone with no crew, no tape, and no date offers a reader nothing to check, so its authority is only what a passing reader grants it.

   Rhythms: a street has a weekly cycle of trash collection, deliveries, and parking turnover, and a slower cycle of maintenance work. A working-site cone belongs to the short rhythm of a job that starts and ends. Three weeks is longer than most small jobs, so the cone is out of step with the rhythm that normally explains it.

   Expectations: residents expect that a marker on their street either has a reason or gets removed by whoever owns it. Each resident can also assume that a neighbor, or the authority, has already dealt with it. That assumption is how one cone can stand for three weeks.

   Competitors for the same resources: the cone competes for the street's shared attention with every other signal, and for its physical space with the parking and driving it may or may not be blocking. It also competes with the simplest explanation, "it's nothing," which costs the reader nothing to hold.

3. Does the next level up from that system change anything for the cone? Answer the first two questions again for the container, and stop at the first level where the cone's purpose is plain or where the next level would change nothing about it.

   Yes, one level up changes something. The container is the neighborhood together with the authority that governs street use and maintenance.

   Feeds it: complaints and reports from residents, and the authority's own inspection rounds and work orders. Takes what it produces: the authority receives, or does not receive, the report that a stray cone exists. Serves: the upkeep of a street that residents can use safely without having to police it themselves.

   What it asks of the cone: that any object in the road be traceable to a person or a job. An untraceable object is a small fault in that system, since nothing routes it to someone able to remove it. The competitor for resources here is every other report the authority receives. A cone that hurts nobody ranks low, so the report that would end its stay tends not to be filed. Neighbors' shared assumption that someone else reported it keeps the rank low.

   The next level up, the wider city or region, changes nothing further about the cone: its rules of street use are the same rules, and no additional demand reaches a single cone. Stop here.

4. What changes about the cone once it is seen in place: its purpose, its priorities, or what counts as success for it?

   Its purpose shifts. Seen alone, the cone is a warning about a hazard. Seen in place, after three weeks with no crew and no owner, it is a test of the street's routing: a small, harmless probe of whether anyone in the system takes responsibility for an unowned object. The cone still works as a signal to a driver, but what the neighbors and authority are learning from it now is how the street handles ownerless things.

   Its priorities shift. A cone is built to be seen and to stay put, and both hold. Once seen in place, the cone's staying put has become the notable fact, not its visibility.

   What counts as success shifts. Alone, success is that a driver steers around it. In place, success is that the cone gets resolved: either an owner explains it, or the authority or a neighbor removes it, or someone confirms the reason it stands. Three weeks of standing means the system has so far had no such resolution.

   Open: the answers to step 1 rest on candidate purposes, because the set does not say what the cone sits next to, whether it blocks anything, or whether any report has been filed. Each fact would narrow the candidates: a nearby hole points to a hazard marker, a nearby empty space to a reserved spot, and a filed report with no reply to a routing failure in the authority.
