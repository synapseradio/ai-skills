## Run

### 1. Four quadrants

**Verified facts**

- You are hosting for 14 people, and the meal is tomorrow. You told me this directly.
- A turkey is safe once its internal temperature reaches 165 °F, measured with a food thermometer in the innermost part of the thigh, the innermost part of the wing, and the thickest part of the breast. (cooking page)
- Set the oven no lower than 325 °F. Preheating is not necessary. Roast breast-side up on a flat wire rack in a shallow pan 2 to 2 1/2 inches deep. (cooking page)
- Let the bird stand 20 minutes before carving. This is for quality, since the juices set. It is not a safety step. (cooking page)
- A frozen turkey thawed in the refrigerator needs about 24 hours per 4 to 5 pounds. A 12 to 16 pound bird takes 3 to 4 days, and a 20 to 24 pound bird takes 5 to 6 days. (thawing page)
- Cold-water thawing takes about 30 minutes per pound, in a leak-proof bag. (thawing page)
- A turkey left on the counter more than 2 hours is at an unsafe temperature. The outer layer sits between 40 and 140 °F while the center is still frozen. (thawing page)
- The fridge-thawed bird can wait 1 to 2 days before cooking. (thawing page)
- The cooking page recommends baking stuffing in a casserole. Its guidance on stuffing the bird is to stuff loosely and to bring the stuffing's center to 165 °F too. (cooking page)

**Known unknowns**

Each is a question I know to ask and cannot answer from what you said.

1. Is the turkey frozen, fresh, or partly thawed right now? This decides everything else. By the thawing page's own figures, a frozen bird cannot thaw in a refrigerator by tomorrow. A 12 pound bird needs 3 days. Cold water for a 20 pound bird is about 10 hours (20 lb x 30 min), and the water must be changed to stay cold, which the page also requires.
2. How many pounds is the bird, and is it one bird or two? I do not know your bird's weight. I also do not know whether one bird is the plan.
3. Does one bird of that weight fit your oven with the rack and pan you own, and does it leave room for the sides?
4. What time do you serve? Roasting time has to be worked backward from that. The cooking page's time chart did not come through in my extraction, so I have no verified minutes-per-pound figure for your weight.
5. Do you own a food thermometer? If not, the one verified doneness test is unavailable to you.
6. Are you stuffing the bird or baking stuffing separately?
7. What else needs the oven tomorrow, and at what temperature?
8. Do any of the 14 have allergies or dietary restrictions that touch the turkey (brine, butter, herbs)?

**Unrecognized knowledge**

This is what you likely hold and may not have applied. I am inferring it from the situation. You have not told me any of it.

- You have eaten turkey dinners before. What you remember about what worked or failed at the table is real evidence about texture and timing.
- If you have roasted a chicken, the method carries over: thermometer in the thigh, rest before carving. Scale and timing are the parts that differ.
- You know your own oven. Whether it runs hot, cold, or unevenly is data no page has.
- You can ask. Someone you know has roasted a large bird for a crowd. A ten-minute call today is a cheap source.

**Blind spots**

A blind spot is a fact you would not think to ask about. I found these by probing with analogy and by asking what a caterer or an experienced host would check that a first-timer skips.

- Analogy, a big-event caterer: work backward from serving time and write the schedule on paper, with thaw, roast, rest, carve, and every side's oven slot. Your question covers the bird and not the timeline.
- Analogy, a pilot's preflight: check equipment before starting. Roasting pan size, rack, thermometer, foil, a cutting board with a juice groove, and a platter big enough for the carved bird are separate failure points.
- Outsider view, a food-safety inspector: cross-contamination is the risk you do not see. Raw turkey juice touches the sink, the counter, and your hands. The state page's advice (retrieved in the first search, not verified further here) is to wash hands, utensils, and surfaces with soap and water after contact.
- Outsider view, a guest with an early flight: the bird may finish earlier than planned. The rest period and a holding plan (foil, a warm spot) are the buffer, and I have no verified holding-time figure.
- Giblets: many birds arrive with a packet inside the cavity. Missing it is a common first-time surprise. I have this as general awareness, not from a checked source.

### 3. The gaps

Gaps that close by looking, today:

- Bird state: touch it. Frozen, or firm with ice crystals, or pliable. Read the label for weight.
- Oven fit: put the empty roasting pan in the oven with the rack you plan to use. Measure the bird's height against the space above the pan.
- Thermometer: find it and check that it reads.
- Giblets: read the label, and check the cavity after thawing.
- Serving time: decide it. This is your choice, and everything else derives from it.

Gaps that close by asking:

- Guests: send one message asking about allergies and restrictions.
- Roasting time for your weight: I did not retrieve the time chart. Read it on the cooking page, or ask a friend who has roasted a bird near your weight.

Gaps that stay as acknowledged uncertainty:

- Exactly when the bird will hit 165 °F. Roasting time varies with oven, pan, and bird. The cooking page says many variables affect it. Plan a window and let the thermometer decide.
- How the meal will taste. No source settles this in advance.

### 4. Readiness against risk

- Cost of being wrong on doneness or thaw: serious. Undercooked poultry is a food-safety failure for 14 people, and a frozen center means a late meal. Demand direct verification here: a thermometer reading, and a touch test on the bird.
- Cost of being wrong on flavor, presentation, or side timing: low. Proceed with acknowledged uncertainty. A bird that is a bit dry or a side that is late costs a bad hour and nothing more.
- Cost of being wrong on oven fit: medium and cheap to verify. Check it today, since finding out at 3 pm tomorrow leaves no options.

Verify the high-consequence items and let the rest ride.

### 5. Close the critical gaps first

In order of how much each would change your plan:

1. **Is the bird frozen?** If yes, start cold-water thawing today, or switch the plan (a fresh bird from a shop, or a thawed one). Source: the thawing page. This decides tomorrow's menu.
2. **What is the weight, and does it fit?** The label gives the weight. The empty-pan test in the oven gives the fit. Source: experiment.
3. **Do you own a working food thermometer?** Buy one today if not. It is the single verified doneness test.
4. **Work the schedule backward from serving time.** Fix the serving time, then subtract the 20 minute stand, the roast time from a chart for your weight, and the oven's spare capacity for the sides.
5. **Ask one experienced person one question:** "What surprised you the first time you roasted a bird for a crowd?" This reaches the blind spots I could not name.

Efficient sources here: the bird's label and your own oven for the technical details, the two pages above for intended behavior, and a test in your own kitchen for actual behavior.

### 6. Limits of competence

- I cannot see your kitchen, your bird, or your oven. Everything about them stays an assumption until you check it.
- I did not retrieve the roasting time chart, so I give you no minutes-per-pound figure. I also give no per-guest portion figure, because I did not verify one.
- I cite the state page on hand-washing only as something I saw in a search snippet. I did not open it.
- Advice on brining, basting, spatchcocking, and other techniques would come from cooking experience and would carry no cited source here. If you want that, ask someone who has done it. Treat anything I add on those as unverified.
- Some questions cannot be settled today: how the bird will taste, and how long your oven will take. Plan a buffer for them, and use the thermometer.
