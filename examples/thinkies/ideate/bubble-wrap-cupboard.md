# ideate: a cupboard full of left-over bubble wrap

- skill: ideate, from commit 0c9258f
- subject: What can we do with a cupboard full of left-over bubble wrap?
- kind: single run

## Prompt

/ideate What can we do with a cupboard full of left-over bubble wrap?

## Run

### 1. Seed

Decomposing the opportunity first. "A cupboard full" splits into four questions.

- What is the stock? Bubble wrap of mixed sizes, probably clean, probably some sheets popped. Volume is large, weight is close to nothing, and it is otherwise worthless to most buyers.
- Who holds it? Someone whose cupboard is now unusable for its own purpose.
- What is "do something" allowed to mean? Reuse, sell, give away, convert into something else, or dispose of properly.
- What does the material do well? It traps air, cushions impact, insulates, diffuses light, resists water, pops for pleasure, and is transparent, flexible and cheap.

**The opportunity.** The cupboard holds a large volume of a light, cushioning, insulating, water-resistant, popping material. The opportunity is to turn that volume into something worth more than the cupboard space it costs, or to free the space in a way that does no harm.

**Hard constraints** (non-negotiable):

- No cost worth noting. The material is free, so any idea that needs a purchase larger than the wrap's value is out.
- No burning it and no putting it in general waste where a recycling route exists. Most bubble wrap is polyethylene film that household kerbside collection often refuses, so disposal has to go through a drop-off point or a reuse route. (Assumed from general knowledge, not checked for the reader's locality.)
- Nothing that puts a person at risk: no wrap near heat sources, no wrap as a substitute for a safety-rated product (a bicycle helmet, a fire blanket).
- The cupboard must be usable again at the end.

**Soft preferences** (nice, not required):

- Uses a lot of wrap at once, since volume is the problem.
- Needs no special tools.
- Makes something someone else wants.
- Is fun.

**Success criteria.** A good idea leaves the cupboard measurably emptier, takes a weekend or less, and either produces something useful or hands the wrap to someone who values it.

**Announced filter for step 4.** An idea survives only if it (a) uses no more than trivial money, (b) has a legal and safe end for the plastic, (c) consumes wrap in bulk, and (d) can start this week.

### 2. Diverge

No evaluation yet. Quantity first.

**SCAMPER**

1. Substitute: use it instead of foam or paper as packing filler for anything the household ships.
2. Substitute: use it instead of a plastic sheet as a window insulator.
3. Substitute: line the base of pots and planters with it instead of gravel, so the pots weigh less and drain.
4. Combine: sandwich it between two layers of cloth to make a padded pet bed, mat or kneeling pad.
5. Combine: fill a duvet-cover shell with wrap for a very light, very loud floor cushion.
6. Adapt: hang it as a frost cover over garden plants, as horticultural fleece is used.
7. Adapt: use it as a greenhouse lining, as commercial growers do for insulation.
8. Modify: cut it into strips and weave a rope or a mat.
9. Modify: melt-seal it (with an iron, baking paper between) into sheets of different shapes; note this is where a fume risk begins.
10. Put to other uses: a diffuser for a lamp or a window, so light scatters and the view is hidden.
11. Put to other uses: a mould for casting plaster, where each bubble imprints a dimple.
12. Put to other uses: a printmaking roller or stamp, dabbed with paint for texture.
13. Put to other uses: a wall-texture tool for painters.
14. Put to other uses: a packing layer for moving house, sold or lent to people who are moving.
15. Eliminate: get rid of the pretence that it is waste, and treat it as a shared resource for a neighbourhood.
16. Reverse: instead of the wrap protecting objects, let the objects protect the wrap. For example, a "bubble wrap shrine": an installation in which the wrap is the artwork.

**Lateral thinking, distant analogies**

1. Bubble wrap as a sound absorber: hang it behind a music stand or in a practice corner to soften echo. (It absorbs little, which is worth testing.)
2. Bubble wrap as a pop-drum: an instrument played by stomping on a large sheet.
3. Bubble wrap as stress therapy: a "pop wall" in a waiting room or an office.
4. Bubble wrap as a cooling jacket for a lunchbox, following the logic of an insulated bag.
5. Bubble wrap as a snow-shoe pad or a skid-pad, laid under a pot to stop it sliding.
6. Bubble wrap as a paddling-pool insulator: line the floor of the pool with it so the water stays warmer and feet are cushioned.
7. Bubble wrap as a child's costume: armour, a dinosaur, a robot.

**Inversion, flipped assumptions**

1. Assume the wrap is more valuable than what it protects: charge a small fee to wrap other people's fragile things.
2. Assume more wrap is better: build a "bubble room", a play space for toddlers with wall-to-wall cushioning.
3. Assume nobody wants it: put it in a "free to good home" list and see who replies.
4. Assume the cupboard is the problem, not the wrap: use the cupboard as a soundproof booth by lining its walls.
5. Assume popping is the point: sell bags of "pre-popped" and "pop-me" wrap.

**Constraint manipulation**

1. Remove the time constraint: keep the wrap as a slow-burn supply for every parcel sent in the next five years.
2. Add a constraint (only outdoors): use it as plant frost cover, paving underlay and a barrow liner.
3. Add a constraint (only in the next hour): hand half to the nearest neighbour who is moving.
4. Add a constraint (zero storage): compress and bag it for a bulk drop-off at a recycling point.
5. Remove the "no purchase" constraint: buy a heat sealer and turn the wrap into insulated envelopes to sell.

**Wonder-driven**

1. What if the bubbles were used as tiny lenses? Each bubble focuses light a little, so a sheet against a window throws a grid of light dots on the floor.
2. What if we ran a pop-count competition at a fete, with the wrap sold by the sheet?
3. What if a school art class made a giant bubble-wrap mural, painted from behind?
4. What if the sheets were stacked as cheap thermal insulation behind a radiator, to reflect heat back to the room (with foil, as radiator panels do)?
5. What if the wrap became a tactile "sensory sheet" for a care home or a special school?
6. What if a wedding or party used it as a confetti-safe floor cover, popped on cue?
7. What if we asked the local theatre or film prop department whether they use it for stage snow, rain effects or armour?

### 3. Cluster

Forty ideas group into six directions by mechanism.

- **A. Pass it on (Give and sell).** Ideas 14, 15, 24, 26, 31, 35, 40. Mechanism: someone else uses the wrap as wrap, or values it for a small fee. Same resource, new owner.
- **B. Insulate (Home and garden).** Ideas 2, 6, 7, 20, 22, 30, 37. Mechanism: trapped air slows heat loss, so the wrap protects windows, plants and pools from cold.
- **C. Pad and soften (Comfort objects).** Ideas 4, 5, 21, 25, 27, 17. Mechanism: air pockets absorb impact and (a little) sound, so the wrap becomes cushions, mats and lined spaces.
- **D. Fill and pack (Logistics).** Ideas 1, 3, 32. Mechanism: light bulk used as filler, in parcels, in pots, or compressed for transport.
- **E. Make things from it (Craft and art).** Ideas 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 16, 34, 36, 33. Mechanism: the wrap is raw material or a texture tool.
- **F. Play with the popping (Play and therapy).** Ideas 18, 19, 23, 28, 38, 39. Mechanism: the pop itself is the value.

Ideas 29 (keep for five years) fits none of these and is a plain hoard; it is carried forward only as a fallback.

### 4. Filter

Checked against the announced filter: (a) trivial money, (b) safe legal end for the plastic, (c) consumes wrap in bulk, (d) can start this week.

**A. Pass it on: Feasible.** No cost, the plastic gets a second life as wrap, and volume leaves the cupboard as soon as someone collects it. It fails only if nobody replies, and that is checkable in a day.

**B. Insulate: Conditional (needs a space to use it).** Window and greenhouse lining and frost cover use a lot of wrap and cost nothing. It is conditional on having a window, greenhouse or garden, and on the wrap being clear and unpopped enough to trap air. Idea 37 (behind a radiator) is eliminated for the reason it touches a heat source, which the constraint on safety rules out. Ideas 20 and 22 survive inside this cluster.

**C. Pad and soften: Conditional (needs a cover and a use).** Bulk use is good, but each object uses only a few sheets, so a cupboard's worth needs many objects. Needs cloth or covers, which are free only if the household already owns spare. Idea 27 (a lined cupboard as a booth) is eliminated: the cupboard would need to be emptied first and the wrap absorbs little sound.

**D. Fill and pack: Feasible.** Zero cost, immediate start, and it consumes wrap at the rate parcels leave the house. Bulk is limited by the shipping rate, so alone it will not empty the cupboard. Idea 3 (pot lining) is feasible and cheap.

**E. Make things from it: Eliminated for the melt-seal and casting variants; Conditional for the rest.** Idea 9 (melt-sealing) fails the safety constraint because heating polyethylene film releases fumes. Idea 33 fails the money constraint. Idea 11 (plaster) needs plaster, which is a purchase. Crafts consume very little wrap per piece, so they fail the bulk test unless run as a group activity (idea 36, a school mural), which is conditional on finding a group.

**F. Play with the popping: Conditional (needs an audience).** A pop wall or fete stall uses a lot of wrap and has almost no cost, but needs a venue and people. Idea 39 (party floor) is conditional on an event. The cluster is fun and it satisfies a soft preference. It is eliminated as a sole strategy because it needs a venue this week.

**Fallback (29, hoard for later): Eliminated.** It fails the success criterion, since the cupboard stays full.

Survivors: A, B, C, D, E (group variant only), F.

### 5. Refine

**A. Pass it on.** Strongest variant: idea 26 combined with 14 and 31. Post a "free bubble wrap, one cupboard's worth" notice on a neighbourhood group, a community noticeboard, and to anyone moving house, and offer collection in bags by size. How it works: people who are packing or posting collect it in a day or two, and remaining wrap goes to a school, a charity shop or a small business that ships parcels. Key risk: people say yes, then never collect. Mitigation is a fixed collection window and a "first to reply, first to collect" rule. What makes it distinct: it is the only direction that empties the cupboard without any making, storing or event.

**B. Insulate.** Strongest variant: idea 2 and 7, wrap on the inside of windows and, where available, a greenhouse or shed. How it works: spray a light mist of water on the glass, press the flat side of the wrap against it, and the surface tension holds it up. The trapped bubbles slow heat loss. Key risk: the wrap yellows and turns brittle in strong sun over a season or two, so it is a winter measure. What makes it distinct: it turns bulk into a measurable benefit, lower heat loss, that the household notices on its own bill. (The claim that bubble wrap insulates glass is an assumption from general practice, not something I measured for this run.)

**C. Pad and soften.** Strongest variant: idea 4, kneeling pads and pet mats. How it works: layer several sheets, stitch or tape inside a cover from old fabric, and hand them out to people who garden or have animals. Key risk: a cushion of wrap is noisy and flattens over time, so it is a stopgap and not a bed. What makes it distinct: it makes an object with a face and a use, which is easy to give as a gift.

**D. Fill and pack.** Strongest variant: idea 1 combined with 32. Keep a working supply for the household's own parcels and returns, and compress the surplus into a few bags for a bulk drop-off where the plastic is accepted. How it works: set aside one small bag per month for parcels and offer the rest through A. Key risk: the drop-off point may not accept it, so this needs a check of local rules first. What makes it distinct: it is the safe disposal route that guards against A failing.

**E. Make things (group variant).** Strongest variant: idea 36, a mural or printing session run with a school, club or library. How it works: painters dab paint on a wrap sheet and press it to paper or fabric, so the bubbles print as a dotted texture. A session uses several large sheets and yields a group artwork. Key risk: paint on plastic wastes the wrap, so it cannot then be reused as wrap. What makes it distinct: it produces something shown to others and pulls in people who would not otherwise look at a cupboard.

**F. Play with the popping.** Strongest variant: idea 19 and 38, a pop wall or sensory sheet at a care home, a school or an office. How it works: tape large sheets to a wall or lay them on the floor for an hour or a week, and let people pop them. Key risk: the noise, and that a sensory setting needs someone who knows what the audience tolerates. What makes it distinct: it is the only direction where the wrap's most familiar behaviour is the whole point.

### 6. Hand off

**Option 1: Pass it on**

- Approach: Offer the whole cupboard free to neighbours, movers, small sellers and schools, with a fixed collection window.
- Strengths: Empties the cupboard fastest with no cost, no making, no storage, and gives the plastic a second life as wrap.
- Key risk: Takers do not turn up, so the cupboard stays full.
- Requires: A neighbourhood channel or noticeboard, a collection slot, and bags sorted by size.

**Option 2: Insulate**

- Approach: Fix wrap to the inside of windows, and to a greenhouse or shed if there is one, for the cold months.
- Strengths: Uses wrap in bulk, costs nothing, and gives a visible benefit in warmth and heating cost.
- Key risk: Sun degrades the wrap within a season, and the result is a winter fix only.
- Requires: Windows or a greenhouse, a water spray, and clear, mostly unpopped sheets.

**Option 3: Pad and soften**

- Approach: Turn layered wrap in old fabric covers into kneeling pads, pet mats and packing sleeves.
- Strengths: Produces an object someone wants, and works as a gift or a stall item.
- Key risk: Wrap flattens and rustles, and each object uses few sheets, so the cupboard barely shrinks.
- Requires: Spare fabric, needle and thread or tape, and some hours.

**Option 4: Fill and pack**

- Approach: Keep a small working stock for parcels and pot linings, and send the surplus to a recycling drop-off that accepts film.
- Strengths: Free, immediate, and it is the guaranteed end for wrap nobody wants.
- Key risk: The nearest drop-off may not take bubble wrap, and household parcels use little.
- Requires: A check of local recycling rules and one trip with the bags.

**Option 5: Make things as a group**

- Approach: Run a printing or mural session with a school, club or library, with the wrap as a texture tool.
- Strengths: Consumes several large sheets per session, makes something shown to others, and brings in people.
- Key risk: Painted wrap cannot be reused as wrap, and the session needs a willing host.
- Requires: Paint, paper or fabric, and a venue and a group.

**Option 6: Play with the popping**

- Approach: Put a pop wall or sensory sheet in a care home, school or office for a set time.
- Strengths: Uses a lot of wrap, costs almost nothing, and turns the wrap's best-known trait into the point.
- Key risk: Noise, and the need for someone who knows what the audience tolerates.
- Requires: A venue, a host, tape, and a time slot.

**Suggested next steps.**

- The options have clear trade-offs on cost, time and audience, so weigh options is the natural next move, and Option 1 backed by Option 4 as a fallback is the pairing to compare against the rest.
- Several factors compete (speed, benefit, effort, fun), so a priority matrix would rank them.
- The stakes here are low, so a premortem is not needed, except on Option 1: run one on "nobody collects it".
- Stakeholders are few, so a perspective shift is optional. If the household is not the only owner of the cupboard, ask what the others want to keep.
