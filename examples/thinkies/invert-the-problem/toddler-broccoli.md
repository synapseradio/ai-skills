# invert-the-problem: getting a toddler to eat broccoli

- skill: invert-the-problem, from commit 0c9258f
- subject: How do I get my toddler to eat broccoli?
- kind: single run

## Prompt

/invert-the-problem How do I get my toddler to eat broccoli?

## Run

### 1. Flip the goal

The stated goal is "get my toddler to eat broccoli." The opposite is "guarantee my toddler refuses broccoli, permanently, and comes to dread it."

Stated that way, the goal already shows something. "Get my toddler to eat" makes the parent the actor and the toddler the object. The flipped goal asks what a toddler does when someone tries to make them do something, and a toddler's most reliable move is refusal. So the real target may not be "broccoli eaten" but "broccoli stops being a contest."

### 2. List failure paths

Every way to guarantee refusal and dread:

- Announce that broccoli is good for them, so it is a chore before it is food.
- Bribe with dessert, so broccoli becomes the toll and dessert the prize.
- Say "just one bite" and watch every bite. Attention turns a bite into a performance.
- Serve it only when the toddler is already starving and cranky, so it arrives with distress attached.
- Serve it boiled soft and grey, with a smell and texture that reads as spoiled.
- Put a large pile on the plate, so the task looks impossible.
- Show visible disappointment or anger at each refusal, so refusal gets a reaction and the reaction is rewarding.
- Hide it in other food and get caught, so the toddler learns that food may be lying.
- Offer it once, take the refusal as final, and never serve it again. (This guarantees never eating it, by another route.)
- Eat something else yourself at the same meal and never touch the broccoli, so the model on show says it is not for people.
- Make the toddler stay at the table until it is gone, so the table becomes a place of conflict.
- Praise loudly when it is eaten, teaching that eating it earns a reward and refusing it is a bid for one.

### 3. Reverse into protections

Each failure path, reversed:

- Chore label: say nothing about health. Put it on the table as ordinary food.
- Bribe: never link dessert to broccoli. Serve dessert, when there is any, on its own terms.
- Watching: look elsewhere. Eat your own meal and talk about something else.
- Hunger and crankiness: serve it early in the meal, or as the first thing out while the toddler is hungry but not spent.
- Grey and soft: roast it until the edges brown, or steam it briefly so it stays bright green. Salt, oil, or a dip changes what it is.
- Large pile: put out one floret, or two.
- Reaction to refusal: keep a flat face and take the plate away without comment.
- Hiding it: never disguise it. Serve it openly, and blend it into food only where the toddler can see it go in.
- Offer once: serve it again and again, in different forms, with no pressure, across weeks. Repeated exposure is the mechanism.
- Absent model: eat it yourself, visibly and with appetite, without commentary.
- Forced stay: let the toddler leave the table when done. Table time ends without a verdict.
- Loud praise: stay quiet when it is eaten, too.

Read together, the protections share a shape: remove the audience, remove the stakes, keep the broccoli present.

### 4. Try other reversals

**Reverse the sequence.** The plan assumes serve, then refuse, then persuade. Reverse it: the toddler meets broccoli before mealtime. It sits in a bowl during cooking, a floret goes into a hand to hold, or it turns up in play as trees for toy animals. Touch and smell come first, and eating comes last, possibly much later. The toddler's first contact with broccoli then carries no demand.

**Reverse the relationship, who acts on whom.** The parent acts on the toddler; flip it so the toddler acts on the broccoli, and on the parent. The toddler washes it, tears florets off the stalk, chooses between two bowls, sprinkles the salt, or serves the parent a piece. A toddler who has dunked, torn, and handed over broccoli has a relationship with it that a fork pushed toward their mouth never builds. Under this reversal the toddler can also feed the parent, and the parent's pleasure at being fed is real and unrehearsed.

**Reverse the optimization target.** The target was "broccoli eaten today." Reverse it to "broccoli never becomes a fight," and then to "the toddler's ease with new foods at age ten." Under those targets a refused meal costs nothing, and a calm refusal counts as a partial success because the toddler stayed at the table and looked at the plate. The measure changes from bites per meal to how the meal felt, across months.

**Reverse the perspective.** Take the toddler's view. A small person has almost no control over what happens to them all day: clothes, car seats, naps, and food arrive by adult decision. Refusing food is one of the few places a toddler's "no" holds. The broccoli is not the enemy. The loss of the one veto is. From there, "get them to eat it" is a request to surrender the last veto, and a parent who leaves the veto intact removes the reason to defend it.

A fifth, smaller reversal: reverse the setting. Serve broccoli where nothing else is going on: at a picnic, at a friend's table with other children eating it, or from a shared platter. Peers and a change of place lower the stakes at once.

### 5. Keep the sharpest insight

The sharpest insight is the perspective reversal, and it reorganizes the rest: **broccoli is refused because the toddler is being asked to concede, and the refusal is the toddler's veto, not a verdict on the taste.** Nearly every failure path in step 2 puts pressure or an audience on that veto. Nearly every protection in step 3 takes it away.

The most actionable form of that insight is a division of labor. The parent decides what is served, when, and in what form. The toddler decides whether to eat it and how much. The parent's whole job is to keep offering, cook it well, eat it in front of the toddler, and stay quiet about the outcome. The toddler's veto stays intact, and the pressure that made refusing worthwhile is gone.

One caveat carries its own reversal: a plan that works only while the parent hides their hope is fragile. If the parent is still privately counting bites, the toddler will read it. The reversal of "get my toddler to eat broccoli" that survives is "get myself to stop needing my toddler to eat broccoli today."
