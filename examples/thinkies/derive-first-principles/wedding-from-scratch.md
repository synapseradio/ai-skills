# derive-first-principles: why weddings cost so much, rebuilt from the ground up

- skill: derive-first-principles, from commit 0c9258f
- subject: Why do weddings cost so much? Rebuild one from the ground up.
- kind: single run

## Prompt

/derive-first-principles Why do weddings cost so much? Rebuild one from the ground up.

## Run

No figures below come from a source. Where a number appears, it is illustrative arithmetic to show the shape of a cost, and the run marks it so.

### 1. Identify the convention

The belief: a wedding is expensive, and the expense is close to unavoidable. The conventional approach bundles a set of items, usually in this order: a venue booked a year or more ahead, a caterer serving a plated or buffet meal to every guest, a photographer and often a videographer, a florist, a band or DJ, a dress and formal clothing worn once, a cake, invitations, a planner or coordinator, and a guest list built from family obligation as much as from closeness.

The question the run answers has two halves. Why does the bundle cost what it costs? And what is the smallest thing that is still a wedding?

### 2. Question every component

For each component, the test is whether it is a fundamental truth, or an assumption from convention, analogy, or authority. Convention is "everyone does it". Analogy is "it is like a gala or a conference". Authority is "the magazine, the planner, or the relative says it is required".

| Component | Fundamental, or inherited? | Source of the belief |
|---|---|---|
| Two people commit to each other | Fundamental. Without it there is no wedding. | Definition |
| A legal act recognizing the commitment | Fundamental where recognition matters (property, immigration, medical decisions, inheritance). It is a form, a witness requirement, and a fee, set by the jurisdiction. Which jurisdiction applies is a fact to look up. | Law |
| Witnesses | Fundamental in the sense that a public commitment needs someone to see it. The number required is set by law and is small. The number invited is not. | Law for the minimum; convention for the rest |
| Guests beyond the witnesses | Real but variable. People want their community to see the commitment and to feel included. That wish is genuine. The size of the guest list is a choice, and every other cost scales with it. | Human need; then convention and obligation |
| A meal | Partly fundamental. Gathered people who travel or stay for hours need food. A three-course plated dinner is not required by that need. | Need; then analogy to formal dinners |
| A venue | Partly fundamental. Gathered people need a place. It does not need to be a dedicated wedding venue. | Need; then convention |
| A date a year out on a Saturday in peak season | Inherited. Nothing about the commitment depends on the weekday or month. | Convention and habit |
| Photography | Partly fundamental. A record has value later. Eight hours of professional coverage is a package, not the need. | Need; then authority (the planner's timeline) |
| Flowers, decor, a cake | Inherited. Pleasant, and none is required by the commitment or the gathering. | Convention, analogy to a stage set |
| A band or DJ | Inherited. Music and dancing serve a want. Live performance is one way among several. | Convention |
| A dress and formal wear bought new | Inherited. Marking the day as special is a real want. The garment does not need to be bought new, or worn once. | Convention and authority |
| A planner or coordinator | A derived cost. The bundle above has many parts and dependencies, and someone must coordinate them. Its existence is the price of the complexity created by the other choices. | Consequence of the bundle |
| The word "wedding" on the invoice | Possibly fundamental to the price, not to the work. See below. | Market structure |

The last row needs its own pass, because it is the answer to the "why so much" half.

**Where does price come from, as distinct from cost?** A vendor's cost to serve an event is labor, food and materials, space, equipment, insurance, and time. A vendor's price is what the buyer will accept. Three features of how weddings are bought separate the two:

- The purchase is rare and singular. A buyer usually makes it once, has no benchmark, and cannot shop across many prior purchases. Rare purchases weaken the buyer's price knowledge.
- The purchase is high-stakes and cannot be redone. A failed wedding day has no retake, so buyers pay to reduce variance. Vendors sell insurance against disaster, and so do their prices.
- The purchase is emotionally loaded. Buyers treat a low price as a warning sign about quality, and often as a signal about how much they care. That gives vendors room to price above their cost.

Whether vendors in fact charge more for the same service when it is labeled a wedding is an empirical claim. The run holds it as a hypothesis, not a finding. It is testable: ask several caterers or venues for a quote for the identical event under two labels, "wedding" and "private party", on the same date. This has not been run here.

**The date and the guest count.** These two variables drive most other costs, so they get one more pass.

- The guest count multiplies every per-person cost: food, drink, chairs, tables, place settings, staff to serve them, and the space to hold them. The cost of a venue scales with capacity. So the guest list is the single largest lever in the bundle.
- The date sets scarcity. Venues and photographers sell a fixed number of Saturdays. Scarce Saturdays in the popular season cost more. Demand is a convention (people prefer weekends and warm months), and the resulting price is a real market effect of a convention.

Illustrative arithmetic, not sourced: if the per-guest cost of food, drink, seating, and service were 100 units and the fixed costs (venue hire, photography, music, attire) were 3,000 units, then 30 guests would cost 6,000 and 150 guests would cost 18,000. The fixed part is the same. The per-guest part grows fivefold. Multiplication by headcount is where a wedding grows, and headcount is set by the guest list.

### 3. Keep what survives

What holds after the questioning:

1. Two people make a commitment. (Definition.)
2. The commitment is made publicly, in front of at least the witnesses the law requires, and is recorded in whatever legal form the jurisdiction asks for. (Law, plus the human need for a public act.)
3. Some people who matter to the couple are present. How many is a choice. (Human need for community.)
4. People who gather for a few hours are fed and have a place to be. (Physical need.)
5. Something marks the day as different from other days. What marks it is a choice. (Human need for ritual.)
6. Something records it. How much is a choice. (Value of memory.)

Six items. Everything else on the conventional list either serves one of these six in one particular way, or sits outside them: the Saturday, the plated dinner, the florist, the live band, the new dress worn once, the planner who exists because of the other choices.

### 4. Reason up

Given only the six, what follows is a wedding built from each need in turn, choosing the cheapest way that still serves it.

**The commitment and its legal form.** The fee and the form are set by the jurisdiction. The cost is a fee and an appointment. The act can take minutes.

**The witnesses and guests.** The legal minimum is set by law. The guests are the people whose presence the couple wants. Suppose 20 people. The number follows from asking, for each name, "would the couple want this person present if nobody expected them to invite them?" That question separates presence the couple wants from presence obligation adds. Every name that goes on the list because of obligation adds per-guest cost and adds nothing to items 1 to 6 for the couple.

**The place.** People need somewhere to be. Candidates that already exist and are cheap or free: a home, a relative's garden, a public park with a permit, a restaurant's private room, a community hall, a place of worship. None of these is a wedding venue, and none charges the wedding premium unless its owner classes the event as a wedding. Choose by capacity for 20, not by prestige.

**The food.** Food serves a need, so the food should be good for the time of day. A meal at midday or a late-morning brunch needs less than a formal evening dinner. Per-guest cost depends on the menu and on service style. Family-style shared plates need fewer staff than plated service. A potluck, or food from a favorite restaurant collected and set out, needs none.

**The marker.** Ritual can be spoken words, an exchange, a piece of clothing, a song. None of these needs a price. Something worn should be chosen for the wearer, and may be borrowed, second-hand, or already owned.

**The record.** The record can be a friend with a good phone or camera, a single hour of professional photography during the ceremony and the first portraits, or a shared album to which guests upload their own photos. The need is a record of the faces and the moment. Eight hours of coverage is a product.

**The date.** Any date the people who matter can attend. A weekday, a shoulder season, or a date on short notice carries less scarcity premium, because the supplier has an open slot and a low-demand day to fill.

**Rebuilt wedding, one version.** Twenty guests. A weekday in a shoulder season. A garden or a restaurant's private room. A shared meal. A borrowed or previously owned outfit. A friend with a camera and one hour of a professional photographer. A licensed officiant, or the registry office. The legal fee. Everything else is left out.

In the illustrative arithmetic above, this is close to the 6,000-unit line, which is the 30-guest case, and lower still, since the fixed costs that dominated (a dedicated venue, all-day photography, a live band, new attire, a coordinator) are mostly absent. The exact figure depends on the place and the menu and is not asserted. The claim is the ratio: the rebuilt wedding is a small fraction of the conventional one, and most of the reduction comes from the guest list and the fixed-cost items, not from cheaper versions of the same items.

### 5. Compare with convention

Set each conventional constraint against what the reasoning-up produced.

**Constraints that are real**

- The legal form and its fee. Real, and set by the jurisdiction.
- Some witnesses. Real, and the number is small.
- People who gather need food and a place. Real. Both scale with headcount.
- Vendors' costs are not zero. Real. Labor, food, and space have prices.
- Scarcity of popular dates. Real as a market effect, though the demand behind it is a convention.
- Some people will be hurt if not invited. Real, as a social cost. It is a cost to weigh, not a fixed number.

**Constraints that are inherited**

- The one-year lead time. Inherited from the practice of booking dedicated venues, which sell out.
- The Saturday. Inherited from habit.
- The large guest list. Inherited from obligation, from a norm that a wedding is an event for the wider family and friends. That norm can be honored or declined.
- The plated multi-course dinner. Inherited from formal-dinner analogy.
- Flowers, a cake, a printed stationery suite. Inherited from convention.
- The live band or a DJ for the evening. Inherited from convention.
- New formal attire. Inherited from convention and authority.
- A coordinator. A consequence of the bundle. A wedding with 20 guests, one place, and one meal has few parts to coordinate.

**The answer to the first question.** Why do weddings cost so much? The run's best estimate is that weddings cost much because of three things that multiply together, and that only some of them are real. Headcount multiplies the per-guest costs. The convention of a dedicated venue, a formal meal, an evening band, and all-day coverage multiplies the fixed costs. The market for a rare, high-stakes, emotionally loaded purchase may add a premium on top (a hypothesis with a stated test, not a finding). Of these three, the first two are choices and the third is partly a fact about the market.

**A tension in the reasoning.** The step from "the guest list is inherited from obligation" to "reduce it" ignores that for many couples, community presence is the point. The run's fifth fundamental, "some people who matter are present", was kept for that reason. A small wedding removes obligation cost, and it also removes some of what the larger event gives. The reasoning shows what is inherited and what is required. It does not show whether the couple should want the larger event. That is a values question for the couple, and the run leaves it there.

### 6. Open new possibilities

With the inherited constraints removed, these become possible, each derived from a fundamental above:

- **Separate the legal act from the celebration.** The legal form needs minutes and a fee. The celebration can happen on a different day, in a different form, at a different scale. Neither needs to carry the other's cost or timing.
- **Stage the celebration.** A small first event for the people the couple most wants present, and a larger, cheaper, informal gathering later (a picnic, an open house). The per-guest cost of the second is low, because it is not a wedding in format.
- **Choose the guest count first and let it set the budget.** The guest list is the largest lever, so decide it before choosing anything else, and let the venue and menu follow from it.
- **Ask what each person on the list adds.** For each name: is this a person whose presence the couple wants, or one obligation adds? The answer moves the largest lever.
- **Buy the record and the meal as separate small items, not as a package.** A package bundles what the couple needs with what convention added.
- **Test the label.** Request quotes for the identical event under two names. If the quotes differ, the premium is a fact and can be avoided by not using the label with the vendor. If they do not differ, drop the hypothesis.
- **Move the date.** Ask each vendor for the price on a weekday and in the shoulder season. The scarcity premium is a number that can be asked for, not a fixed feature of the service.
- **Give guests a role.** Guests can bring food, music, flowers, or photographs. This turns a per-guest cost into a contribution, and it serves the fundamental of community presence better than paying strangers to supply the same items.
- **Check the fundamentals with the people who matter.** The run kept six. Another person might keep seven, for example a religious rite, a family tradition, or a particular place. The method survives that; the list is theirs to write.

Open question left for the couple: which of the six do they value most, and would they spend on that one instead of on the conventional bundle?
