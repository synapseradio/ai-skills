# wonder: Why do people talk to their cars?

- skill: wonder, from commit 0c9258f
- subject: Why do people talk to their cars?
- kind: single run

## Prompt

/wonder Why do people talk to their cars?

## Run

### 1. Notice the surprising

Three things stand out.

- The car cannot hear. A person says "come on, come on, don't do this to me" to an engine that has no microphone pointed at the driver and no way to act on the words. The speaker knows this, and the speech comes out anyway.
- The talk clusters at moments of friction. Nobody narrates a smooth commute to the dashboard. The words arrive when the engine will not turn over, when the light stays red too long, when the car ahead brakes for no reason, or when the wrong turn has already happened.
- The speech is often kind. People coax, thank, and apologize ("sorry, girl, I know it's cold"). A tool that fails us usually gets cursed, and a car often gets pleaded with.

The surprise underneath all three: an object with no ears receives the same social behavior we give a person, at the very moments when the behavior can do the least.

### 2. Ask generative questions

- What if the words are not addressed to the car at all? Who else could they be for: the speaker, a passenger, an imagined witness, the empty air of a small room?
- What might happen inside the driver if the talk were forbidden? Would the frustration have anywhere to go?
- How might a car be built to answer, and would the talking then feel the same? What if a car replied "I'm doing my best"? Would people keep talking, or would the spell break because an answer makes it a conversation with a stranger?
- What if the car is a room and not a tool? People mutter to themselves in kitchens and offices. A car is a small private room that also moves, so is the speech about the room?
- What might change if the car were shared, a rental or a company van? Would the talk fade, or would it move from "you" to "this thing"?
- How might a person's history with the car matter? Does a car driven for fifteen years get more speech than one driven for fifteen days? Does the speech follow the relationship, or the repairs?
- What if talking to the car is a small rehearsal, a way to hold a feeling in words before it becomes an action like honking or gripping the wheel harder?

### 3. Follow the threads

Start with the thread that the words may not be for the car.

If the speech is for the speaker, it does a job for the speaker. One candidate job: naming a feeling shrinks it. "Come on" says "I am impatient and I am staying calm." A second candidate: speech is a way to hand off a problem. Saying "don't do this to me" places the failure outside the self, which is easier to bear than "I did not maintain this."

That second candidate leads to blame. A car is a good target for blame because it is blamable in a low-stakes way. Nobody is hurt when the car is scolded. So one question follows: do people talk to objects only when blame or hope is free of consequences? Would anyone plead with a bridge or a bank?

The bank question leads sideways to what makes an object addressable at all. A car moves on its own, has a front that looks like a face, makes sounds that change with its state, and runs on hidden processes the driver cannot see. Those are features people tend to read as agency. Which one matters most? Testing the question: people also talk to boats, printers, and stubborn jars of pickles. The jar has no face and makes no sound, so the face and the noise cannot be the whole story. Both the printer and the car hide their inner workings and fail when it matters. Hidden mechanism plus stakes plus dependence starts to look like the shared thread.

Dependence leads to a thread I did not expect. The talk often comes from someone who has no other lever. The engine will not start and the driver has already tried the key. Speech is the only move left that costs nothing. So maybe the talk is what people do at the edge of their control, and the car is the most common place where ordinary adults reach that edge alone, with time to fill.

Alone leads back to the room. If a car is a private room, then talking to it belongs with talking to a dog, a plant, or the television. What would separate those cases from talking to a car? The car also can punish the speaker (an accident), so the speech may carry a trace of bargaining that a plant never earns.

Bargaining opens the last thread: superstition. "Just get me home and I'll get you serviced." Is that a promise to the car, or a promise to a world the car stands in for? The car might be the nearest visible face of a system (machinery, luck, weather, other drivers) that cannot be addressed directly.

### 4. Treat confusion as signal

Confusion 1: the talk persists after the speaker knows it is pointless. If it were only a mistake, it would stop once noticed. It does not stop. So the speech must be paying for itself in some way other than being heard. That is where the attention belongs, on what the speaker gets, and not on whether the car listens.

Confusion 2: the talk is often affectionate, yet the same person would never thank a hammer. Affection and thanks suggest the car is placed in a category closer to companion than tool. What moves an object from one category to the other? The candidates so far: shared time, shared risk, and a history of small rescues. I cannot yet say which of these is required and which only helps.

Confusion 3: I do not know how common this is, or whether it varies by culture, age, or how long a person spends driving. Every claim above about "people" is a guess drawn from the pattern of a familiar scene, not from a study. The gap deserves attention because a guess about frequency would change which explanation looks strongest. If nearly everyone does it, a general explanation about minds fits. If only some do, a story about individual temperament or history fits better.

### 5. Start from uncertainty

What the run holds so far, marked by strength:

- Likely: the talk works for the speaker (naming a feeling, handing off blame, filling the time of a helpless moment), because speech that persists after it is known to be unheard must be doing some other work.
- Plausible, untested: cars are addressable because they move, sound responsive, hide their mechanism, and matter enough to fear. The pickle jar shows a face and a noise are not required.
- Open: whether the talk is a rehearsal of action, a small bargain with a larger unseen system, or both.
- Unknown: how widespread it is, and what changes it.

Questions worth carrying forward, each one a test:

- Do people talk more to a car they cannot afford to lose than to one they can replace?
- Does the talk drop in a car with a voice assistant, or rise?
- Do drivers who commute alone talk more than drivers who commute with others, and does talk to the car turn into talk to the passenger when one is present?
- What do the words say when the driver is happy? Is there any speech to a car that is not about trouble?
- Would a person confess to it if asked, or does it tend to go unreported because it feels silly?

The uncertainty is a starting position. The next step for anyone who wants an answer is the cheapest question first: catch the next time you do it, and notice what you were feeling one second before the words came out.
